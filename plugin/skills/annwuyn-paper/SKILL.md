## Research proposal

A proposal demonstrates topic relevance and significance, justifies the proposed processes and
structures, and presents an implementation plan. It is **not a finalized version**. For
inexperienced researchers, replication research is a legitimate route.

**Preparation:** topic selection → background reading → **gap identification** → expert advice
on topic viability → research questions.

**Structure:** Title page · Abstract · Introduction · Literature review · Research questions ·
Methodology · Limitations · Implications.
(Fuller version, Pajares, `S33 materials/`: Introduction and theoretical framework · Statement
of the problem · Purpose of the study · Review of the literature · Questions and/or hypotheses ·
Design — methods and procedures · Limitations and delimitations · Significance of the study ·
References · Appendixes.)

### Introduction
Pique interest: **why this research?** Then narrow: broad theoretical framework → narrowed-down
scope. State the research type (quantitative vs qualitative).
> *Problems L2 learners encounter in writing* → *Negative transfer of L1* → *L1 vs L2
> preferences for rhetorical patterns.*

An effective problem statement answers *why does this research need to be conducted?* — if you
cannot answer that clearly and succinctly, the statement will read as ambiguous. State the
problem so it **stands out**; obscure problems get masked in extended discussion.

In quantitative studies theory is used deductively and placed early — the objective is to test
or verify it. In qualitative inquiry it depends: grounded theory lets it emerge; otherwise
identify and discuss it early as in quantitative work.

### Literature review
Evidence of theoretical background knowledge, citing relevant studies. Justify the **choice of
methodology** — e.g. textual: Theme/Rheme vs Discourse Topic; interpersonal: Appraisal vs Stance
and Engagement.

**Gap identification** — methodological problems of existing studies · genre bias (e.g. a
preponderance of narrative over argumentative texts) · non-existence of studies on the specific
pairing you propose. The gap brings up the research questions.

Demonstrate a comprehensive grasp of the field and awareness of recent substantive and
methodological developments; show how your study will refine, revise or extend what is known.
**Avoid** statements implying that little has been done or that what has been done is too
extensive to summarise — both read as unfamiliarity with the literature. In a proposal the
review is brief and to the point.

The literature review is also where you justify apparently arbitrary decisions — coding
categories, thresholds, what counts as "frequent". Never leave an unquantified term
(*thường*, *hay*, *có học*) undefined.

### Research questions
**SPECIFIC and OPERATIONALIZABLE.** Narrow every term:

> *How do English essays composed by Vietnamese writers compare with those by American writers?*
> - written texts → discourse features → textual features → **thematic progression**
> - English essays → English argumentative essays → **TOEFL argumentative essays**
> - Vietnamese writers → ESL Vietnamese adult learners → **of intermediate level**
> - American writers → **American expert writers**

Then choose the form:
> Qualitative — *…**HOW** do American expert writers differ from Vietnamese writers in terms of
> frequency of Rheme-Theme patterns?*
> Quantitative — *…do American expert writers use Rheme-Theme patterns **WITH GREATER
> FREQUENCY** than Vietnamese writers?*
An inferential quantitative question states its **hypothesis in advance**; this is the course’s
working test for “quantitative”. Descriptive quantitative questions (how many, what proportion)
need no hypothesis, and qualitative research is defined by its aim of understanding meaning and
process (`references/14-lecture-notes.md` §13).

Hypotheses come in four forms: literary null · **operational null** (generally preferred) ·
literary alternative · operational alternative. Use the null when theory suggests no
relationship; the alternative when theory suggests a direction. Be prepared to interpret **any**
outcome. Keep terminology excruciatingly consistent, and use the same wording and word order
across parallel hypotheses.

### Variables
- **Independent** — the value being manipulated (e.g. essay group: ESL / MV / MA)
- **Dependent** — the observed result (e.g. RT %, MN %)
- **Confounding** — must be controlled: age, gender, education/social background and other
  demographics; language proficiency; learning motivation; curriculum → use a control group,
  or control statistically, or randomize (and state the randomizing unit).

### Methodology
Instruments and materials (surveys, interview protocols, perceptual tests) · subjects and
sampling · data collection and treatment procedures. Indicate the methodological steps that
answer **every** question or test **every** hypothesis.

Sampling turns on **representativeness**: how representative is the sample of the survey
population, and the survey population of the target population? A convenience sample needs an
explicit rationale and stated limitations. Outline sample characteristics; detail informed
consent, anonymity and confidentiality. Justify the sample size: a power analysis or precision
target for quantitative designs, saturation or information power for qualitative ones. Copying
the proportion another study sampled is no justification.

Instrumentation: if previously used, cite reliability and validity findings; if not, outline how
you will develop and test them — a pilot study is nearly essential. Append the instrument.

Analysis procedures: the coding system · interrater reliability (simple % agreement, Cohen's
Kappa) · statistics — test for normality (Kolmogorov-Smirnov), then parametric (t-test, ANOVA,
MANOVA, Pearson's) for normal data, non-parametric (Kruskal-Wallis, Spearman's Rank) otherwise.
Name your tools. Give a rationale for the design, methodology and analyses chosen. Check model
assumptions with plots as well as tests: with large samples, normality tests flag trivial
departures, and with small ones they miss real ones. Report effect sizes with confidence
intervals (`references/13-research-update.md` §5). The lectures’ worked cases on confounders,
control groups, double-blind grading, coding and double data entry: `14` §13.

### Limitations and delimitations
**Limitations** — weaknesses in instruments, sampling and analysis; threats to internal validity
you could not avoid. **Delimitations** — how the study is bounded and *why*: the literature you
will not review, the population you are not studying, the procedures you will not use. Limit
these to things a reader might reasonably expect you to do.

### Implications / significance
The new knowledge the study will produce · the significance of that knowledge · possible
contributions to the discipline. Address both audiences — practitioners and professional peers.
Ask: what do the results mean for the theoretical framework? What subsequent research do they
suggest? What will be improved or changed?

### Abstract
~250 words, and treat that as an economy-of-language exercise. Short introduction to the topic ·
research motivations · method summary · possible implications. Title concise but
self-explanatory. **Use the future tense** in a proposal.

**Title pattern:** `Theme : Aspect [NP] : Goal [NP]`
> *Waste Mineral Filler as a Conventional Mineral Filler Substitute in HMA: A Feasibility
> Assessment*
> *Majority-Minority Discrimination: Ramifications on Ethnic Identity & Self-Esteem*

## Papers

The proposal sections map onto IMRaD; the language work is the same throughout. Section-specific
notes:

- **Introduction** — the funnel is a linearity structure. Sieve it: what is the broad frame,
  what is the narrowed scope, where is the gap, what is the question? Any of these missing is a
  gap `(*)`, not a stylistic choice.
- **Literature review** — the commonest defect is a chain of `N` (New Theme) transitions: one
  study per sentence with nothing linking them. Rebuild as subgroups (by approach, by finding,
  by methodological stance), each internally linear, and use ERT to return to an earlier thread.
  See the worked example in `references/02-thematic-progression.md` (the vocabulary-acquisition
  paragraph, S9) — it is exactly this problem, twice solved.
- **Methods** — sequence vs cause/effect must be marked correctly; check every *then*.
- **Results** — qualification is load-bearing. Every claim scoped to what the data supports;
  see `references/07-modality-qualification.md`.
- **Discussion** — Engagement calibration matters most here: hetero:mono ↑,
  postulation:opinion ↑, specific-source ↑, pronounce ↓. Add pre-counterargument concessions
  before stating limitations.
- **Abstract** — economy of language, hardest constraint, do it last.

Phrase banks by section — two, used differently:

| Section | Morley, *Academic Phrasebank* (tr.) — organised by **move** | Wallwork (phrase no.) — organised by **section** |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract | aims 17–19 · method 19–20 · findings 50 · significance 51–52 | 1, 5 (+2–4) |
| Introduction | territory 7–8 · problem 9 · gap 11–17 · aim/RQ 17–19 · outline 21 | 1–16 |
| Literature review | 22–31, esp. several-authors 23–24 and synthesis 29–30 | 4, 9–16 |
| Methods | 32–37 | 17–30 |
| Results | 38–42 | 29–40 |
| Discussion | 43–48 | 35–45 |
| Conclusions | 49–56 | 45–51 |
| Cross-cutting | critical 58–64 · cautious 65–68 · causality 81–84 · transition 87–89 | cross-references 53–57 |

Wallwork is in the course folder (`S31 materials/Academic vocabulary — Useful phrases for writing
research papers.pdf`); Morley is read from the user's own copy via `scripts/phrasebank.py` in the
`annwuyn` skill. Use Morley when you know the **move** you need (a gap, a concession, a tentative
cause); use Wallwork when you know the **section** and want its conventions. Either way, apply
`references/12-phrasebank.md` → *Choosing between candidate frames*, and never let a frame
assert more than the sources do.

## Current practice the course predates

Details and sources: `references/13-research-update.md` §5, §8 and §9.

- **Introductions** follow Swales’s moves: establish the territory, establish the niche, present
  the work. Prefer a gap that matters to the reader, and consider problematization (challenging
  an assumption in the literature), which tends to yield more interesting questions than
  gap-spotting.
- **Questions:** run FINER on every question; PICOT for intervention studies; SPIDER for
  qualitative and mixed-methods review questions. They complement the course’s *specific and
  operationalizable* rule.
- **Reporting:** follow the EQUATOR guideline for the design (APA JARS, CONSORT 2025, SPIRIT
  2025, PRISMA 2020, RTARG) and the target journal’s open-science requirements (preregistration,
  data availability, CRediT roles).
- **Evidence language:** a *p*-value says nothing about the size or importance of an effect;
  report effect sizes with confidence intervals. Keep causal wording and recommendations at the strength of the design,
  and check every abstract conclusion against the primary outcome (spin).
- **AI disclosure:** when annwuyn edits a manuscript, remind the user once of the journal’s rule;
  under ICMJE and COPE guidance AI cannot be an author, and its use is disclosed.
- **Reasoning drills from the lectures** (variables, confounders, control groups, the “so what?”
  test for questions, narrowing with stated reasons): `references/14-lecture-notes.md` §13.

## Working procedure

1. **Establish the chain before the prose.** Section by section: sieve, consolidate, unpack
   gaps, re-order. Show the chain.
2. **Draft**, applying `03`–`05` (cohesion, dependency, scope/distance/explicitness) as you go.
   Name each paragraph's moves first; realise a weak or missing move from `12-phrasebank.md`
   (move → function → frame → fit → trim).
3. **Calibrate stance** with `06` and `07`.
4. **Compress** with `08`. Report the word count.
5. **Audit the argument** with `09` — surface every implicit warrant; check the fallacy list,
   especially Correlation, Post hoc, Common cause, Sufficiency vs Necessity, Sampling error and
   Composition/Division, which are the ones that bite in research prose.

## Rules

- **Never invent** data, results, citations, sources or numbers. If a claim needs a source the
  user has not given, mark it `(*)` and ask.
- **Never overstate.** If the design does not support a causal claim, do not let the prose make
  one — that is the Correlation fallacy wearing a lab coat.
- Keep terminology consistent across questions, hypotheses, methods and results.
- Explanations in the user's language; the paper itself in English.
