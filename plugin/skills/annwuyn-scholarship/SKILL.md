# annwuyn — scholarship application

Module III of the source curriculum. Load the `annwuyn` skill first; the language layer applies to
every document here, and `references/11-vn-transfer.md` items 18–25 are specific to this genre.

**Order matters:** self-assessment → CV → SOP → LOR → writing sample → package → professor
contact → interview. Each stage feeds the next. Do not draft an SOP before the CV exists.

Even for someone with no concrete plans yet, this process is worth running: taking stock of a
life and career, planning ahead, learning to present oneself, developing motivation for further
study, and supporting family and peers.

---

## 1. Self-assessment

Template: `S15 materials/Scholarship Application — Self Assessment.pdf`.
Fill it before anything else. Sections: Education (current/target degree, target
country/scholarship, GPA vs required minimum, low-GPA mitigations — class standing, major GPA,
GPA conversion, test scores, local graduate courses, professor input; major switching and its
history; necessary preparations) · Research experience (activities, grants, substantial papers)
· Teaching experience · Other skills (language, statistics, major-specific; target scores
checked against past class profiles and cut-offs) · Work experience (per job: key
responsibilities, accomplishments, skills acquired, employer testimonials, potential LOR
references) · Extracurricular and community activities · Honors and awards · Personal
development (changes over the last 5 years; stories illustrating leadership, teamwork,
analytical and critical skills, originality) · Future goals (career short/long-term; academic —
why a graduate degree, why now) · Other potential issues (résumé gaps).

## 2. Academic résumé / CV

Sections: Objectives · Research interests/concentrations · Contact information · Education ·
Relevant experiences (teaching / research / professional) · Publications and conference
presentations · Honors, awards and grants · Extracurricular, community, advocacy ·
Professional affiliations · Other qualifications · Personal hobbies · References.
No single correct format — customise. Research-oriented ≠ work-oriented.

**Objectives** — concise but specific; goal matching → duty prioritization → hierarchical
dependency. *Seeking a professor position in a well-known university* → *an academic faculty
position (tenure track) in an accredited tertiary institution*. Specify size / nationality /
industry. Focus on the destination, not the passion narrative: *Seeking a Master's degree in
Public Relations (with financial aid)*, *…with a planned continuation to Ph.D.*

**Research interests** — 3–6 keywords, or a summary of the proposed research. Not *Software
engineering* (too broad), not *Applied GIS in Forest Resources and Management* (too narrow).
Drives the professor-matching process; critical for PhD applicants.

**Education** — reverse chronological, degrees before certificates, postsecondary only,
certified courses only. Include graduation date (expected date if pending), degree type and
major, department and university, GPA (overall vs major) and grade descriptor, class standing
**put in perspective** (*2nd in my section of 30 (top 10 % in the graduating class of 156)*),
thesis title, relevant courses if switching majors.

**Teaching** — date range, role (teacher/co-teacher/TA/tutor), subject, student level,
enrollment, institution and supervisor.

**Research** — date range, project title, position and duties, supervisor and institution,
significant results (presentations, publications by self and others).

**Publications** — academic research papers only, in the discipline's citation standard (APA or
MLA), your name in bold for multi-author work, Vietnamese titles with an English translation.

**Working experience** — full-time, internships, administrative/part-time/summer; paid only
(except unpaid internships). Date range, job type, position, company, supervisor's contact,
major responsibilities and **accomplishments**. Apply **duty prioritization** to show direction
and progression (*teaching → material adaptation & lesson plan development → curriculum &
textbook development*). **Quantify and put in perspective**: *designed and implemented test
plans (7 major test schemes in 2 years, average rate: 2 schemes/year/tester)* — not
*highly appreciated by the supervisor for professionalism*.

**Language use** — economy of language (NPs, not full sentences); no redundancy or CV padding
(*Intern, duty: Learn to work in a real working environment*); avoid vague quantifiers and
verbs (*some*, *a couple of*, *feel*, *seem*); avoid subjective words, use what hard evidence
backs; but not *too* specific — MM/YYYY suffices. **Avoid name-dropping.**
Action verbs: `S38 materials/Language use — Sample list of action verbs.pdf`.

## 3. Statement of Purpose

Also: letter of interest/motivation/intent, personal statement, admission essay. It promotes
your qualities to the admission committee, serves as a reflection of intellect and personality
(**a personal story**), and is **evidence of your writing and communicative skills**.

Generic structure, customisation expected:

1. a "catchy" but relevant introduction
2. personal history review (pre-college · college · post-college)
3. important academic & professional goals and their relationship
4. reasons for applying to this country / university / program
5. financial needs
6. possible contributions to the program and to the home country
7. post-graduation plans
8. **damage control**

**Introduction** — an interesting hook if you have one. Avoid clichés; *no worthy story ≪ NO
story*. Avoid opening with a well-known quote. Maintain relevance throughout.
**If the word limit is 500 or less, skip the introduction and go straight to the main points.**

**Personal history** — selectivity and relevancy: only experiences that shape you into a good
fit. Pre-college: a personal touch. College: gained knowledge, subjects of interest, relevant
academic and especially research activities, how the research interests **formed and
developed**. Post-college: employment history, the links between academic and professional
activity, justification of any major/career change.

Mention achievements **and failures** — but not in a CV-like way. **Focus on processes**:
problem identification → challenges → specific solutions → results (in perspective) →
implications and lessons learned. Include unique experiences that set you apart.

**Goals** — academic: why graduate study abroad (methodology issues, course availability), why
Master's vs PhD, why now (current problems in work and research). Professional: short-term
(2–5 yrs), long-term (5–10 yrs, important to ODA scholarships).

**Sense of purpose** — the single thing that most distinguishes a strong SOP. Strengthen it by
aligning the whole chain: research interests ↔ academic activities ↔ professional activities ↔
academic goals ↔ professional goals; strong commitment to the target program; specific goals,
not clichés; process-orientation.

**Reasons for applying** — why this country (relationship between the two countries and possible
lessons; important to ODA scholarships); why this program and university. The common mistake is
a one-fit-all generic paragraph. Show you did the research: **specific courses and specific
professors**, and specifically how the program addresses your current problems and career goals.

**Financial needs** — from full scholarship to tuition reduction; list relevant experience and
on-campus employment opportunities. Put yourself on sale.

**Contributions** — to the home country (put in perspective) and to the program (how your
background adds to their diversity).

**Damage control** — justify low scores and GPA, career changes and setbacks, no-activity gaps.

### SOP vs PS

A **PS** is a memorable personal story — compelling, heart-felt, engaging, giving a glimpse of
who you are for better or worse; evidence of a unique voice and potential to make a difference.
Where a school asks for both, read their two prompts carefully: they usually want *academic and
research background + career goals + fit + named faculty* in the SOP, and *the journey, background and
challenges that motivated the decision* in the PS.

The most common PS failure: reading like a bad TV drama — contrived plots, melodramatic tone,
incorrectly used SAT words. *The very day miracle conferred me the opportunity to alter my
destiny and find my long-lost inner self.* Being personal ≠ being literary.

### Top 10 SOP mistakes
1 repeating the application form · 2 not writing to the specific university · 3 boring
introduction · 4 too many points instead of one well-developed one · 5 revealing nothing about
yourself · 6 not proofreading (including another university's name) · 7 forced humour ·
8 pretending to be someone else · 9 not answering the question asked · 10 writing it at the
last moment.

### Post-writing check
Who should read it? Not necessarily an English teacher. You want someone who (a) has an
analytical and critical mind to detect logical and linking problems, (b) has graduate-level
academic and research experience to judge fit, and (c) knows you well **and not that well**.

Peer-review questions: Does the opening capture attention? Does it hold interest throughout?
Does it answer the prompt? Anything inappropriate? Anything irrelevant? Do you feel you know the
writer? Anything unique and distinctive? Does the general tone sound positive?

**Main points:** be personal but analytical · showcase critical thinking, linear writing and
economy of language · avoid logical flaws at all cost · product-focused résumé vs
**process-focused** SOP · balance past accomplishments and future plans · show a strong sense of
purpose · **LINKING-LINKING-LINKING**.

## 4. Letters of Recommendation

Myth: all LORs are more or less the same, just say something nice.
Reality: **LORs should be unpredictable but believable**, and *a mediocre letter is the worst
thing that could be said about an applicant*.

**References** — professors and academic advisors who taught you, supervised your thesis or
research; direct work supervisors (paid jobs and community work). Consider current vs former
boss; academic vs employment letters depending on length of work experience and target degree.
A high-ranking reference needs justification — **a reference should know the candidate
personally**.

**Outline:** self-introduction and purpose · context in which the reference knows the candidate
(state explicitly how long and how well; summarise *all* relevant context) · strengths,
supported by specific and personal examples · weaknesses (sometimes strengths in disguise) ·
potential contributions · personal background, hardships, financial need · self-qualification as
a reference · level of recommendation and success prediction · willingness to communicate ·
**damage control**.

**Qualities to cover** — spread and balance these across the SOP and three LORs; do not cram
every trait into one letter:
analytical (incl. statistical), critical and problem-solving skills, thinking on one's feet ·
learning attitude and aptitude, specialized knowledge · research passion, originality, capacity
for independent research · teaching (esp. PhD) and communication (esp. MBA) · sense of purpose,
work attitude and ethics · teamwork, leadership, interpersonal skills · unique voice and
potential to make a difference · time management and working under pressure · language skills ·
personality (humour, kindness, honesty) · willingness to give back.

**The dominant failure for Vietnamese candidates: unsupported strengths.** *With her
intelligence, she quickly approaches scientific information and creates smart solutions* — in
what way? Replace with observed specifics. Compare the MIT guidance: evaluations are most
helpful when **specific and storied**; *a story or incident that conveys character is more
telling than a mere statement like "Mary is mature."* MIT's questions are a good drafting
checklist — context of the relationship; willingness to take intellectual risks; unusual
competence, talent or leadership; what motivates and excites them; how they interact with
teachers and peers; what you will remember most; why this school is a match; how they reacted to
disappointment or failure; unusual family or community circumstances.

**Make the candidate stand out** by: being selective · putting achievements in perspective
(*one of few staffers conferred the Employee of the Year award in two consecutive years, and the
only one to achieve it right after his first year*) · showing solutions to a persisting problem
· showing that the candidate **makes a difference**.

**Do not dìm hàng** — never elevate the candidate by putting down their peers, their references,
or the home education system.

**Other points:** most important elements first · language errors are acceptable and expected,
provided they do not interfere with meaning · **diversity** of content, vocabulary, style and
form of address across the three references (translation helps) · a personal tone, using
interpersonal Themes sparingly and adding personal reflection, to show the reference cares ·
audience awareness — check that a sentence cannot be read as damning
(*He rarely appeared on the class…*).

Peer-review questions: In what ways does the candidate stand out? Are strengths backed by
specific evidence? Do you feel the reference cares? Suggestions?

## 5. Writing sample

An academic paper (translation if necessary): a published article · a presented paper (better if
in the proceedings) · a thesis or selected chapters · a research proposal · a term paper · a
research project report · a professional project report · a review of a published article or
book. Normally expected of, if not required for, PhD applicants. Have an expert in the field
look it through. Two papers can work — quantitative vs qualitative, or original vs review.

## 6. Application package

Professional and well-thought-out. Print online components for the offline package. Number each
component; header on every page (full name + program + application ID) to prevent mix-ups;
sign or initial each page. Have someone with an eye for detail double-check.

**Cover letter** explains the package contents — template in
`S38 materials/Package — Sample cover letter.pdf`.

Include as applicable: résumés (a compact version and a longer one for interviewers) · SOP/PS ·
unofficial score copies (official reports direct from ETS) · official transcripts (sealed;
explain if your Vietnamese university cannot issue sealed envelopes) · **grading-system
explanation** (VEF GPA conversion table, MOF/FTU grading, US Embassy grade explanation; WES
credential evaluation where required — `wes.org`, free iGPA calculator) · list of references ·
thesis abstract · writing samples · evidence of certificates, testimonials, memberships and
awards · optional multimedia CD (include a clip of your English presentation).

**Translation** — provide your own whenever possible; notarised translation is usually not
required for course certificates, employer testimonials, professional memberships and awards.
The course provides a phrase bank for Vietnamese credentials: certificate-of-merit wordings,
*tốt nghiệp loại giỏi* → *graduated with honors | with high distinction*, *thủ khoa/á khoa* →
*Valedictorian/Salutatorian*, *học sinh giỏi quốc gia* → *a National Merit student*, *trường
chuyên* → *high school for gifted students*, *chương trình tiên tiến* → use the official name +
an explanatory note (*an equivalent of Honors programs in the U.S.*), *lớp trưởng* → *class
monitor*, *bí thư đoàn* → *class liaison officer*, prize names, *giải khuyến khích* →
*consolation prize*, *giải nhì/ba* → *first/second runner-up*. Avoid the word *Olympic* for
national subject contests. See the S38 deck.

**Financial statement** — list requirements and costs (tuition, fees, books, health insurance,
accommodation and board, personal expenses) and available sources (personal savings, family or
sponsor, government/employer, public scholarships, loans, university funds, other income), with
documented evidence (bank affidavit, sponsor affidavit, award letters). If funds are
insufficient, state the need-based aid required and why you deserve it.

**Language use across the package:** positive and assertive, **no hedging**.
*If I have a chance to join the graduate research at…, I would like to work with…* →
*Looking forwards to my graduate research, I'd like to work with…*
*Having proved my ability, I was accepted to join the research group of Dr. Yem* →
*My pet projects drew attention, and Dr. Yem sought me out and asked me to join his research
group, which was a great honor because very few final year students are invited each year…*

## 7. Professor contact

Why professors rather than the graduate school: faculty matching and their say in funding
decisions. (MBA and undergraduate applicants → current students and alumni.) Target junior
professors, professors with research grants, Vietnamese professors overseas, visiting professors.

**Prep:** personal website and online CV, research articles/books/invited talks, dissertation
topics of recent advisees, insights from current students; have your CV ready. Shape your
research interests and SOP accordingly.

**First email:** attention-catching but relevant subject (*Prospective student: a question for
Professor X* / *Enquiry on Fall 2027 admission* from a funded student) · self-introduction and
purpose · brief but **specific** reasons for interest in their work · a response request.
Beware the SPAM risk with personal email; a second attempt after a week, marked
`[second attempt]`. **Do not mention financial aid right away** — but *do* mention immediately
if you are backed by an external scholarship. Avoid TMI; attach the CV unobtrusively
(*FYI, my resume is available online at…*); do your homework and get to the point.

**Subsequent emails:** subject *From (first name) re…* · ask about articles of interest,
research opportunities and guidance, funding · specific, relevant, appropriate questions, one
point at a time, each answerable in reasonable time · no personal questions · nothing readily
available online (consult the Graduate Student Handbook first) · clarification requests are
fine · mind timing and frequency (holidays, start and end of semester) · keep them posted on
your application progress.

## 8. Scholarship interview

Purpose: confirm the candidate is genuine, has a strong sense of purpose, is knowledgeable about
the field, and can communicate orally; clarify inconsistencies (résumé gaps, transcript
irregularities, career setbacks, major changes).

**Preparation is the key; multiple rehearsals are a must.** Review: thesis, ongoing projects,
proposed graduate topics, a glossary of technical terms **and their correct pronunciation**,
"hot" research areas; the school's and professor's work; your own application package, with
specific examples ready for positive traits and for relevant processes. Practise chalkboard
technique. Prepare your own questions, differentiated for academic vs non-academic interviewers.

**Overall strategy:** show that you approach a problem **systematically** — don't answer
immediately; buy time for multi-part answers (*May I have a minute to gather my thoughts?*); jot
down main points; **always start by identifying the problem**. Show leadership potential by
starting with the big picture. Use flowchart and chalkboard techniques for complex concepts.
Demonstrate qualities with specific stories, **focusing on the process**. Subtly guide
interviewers toward what you want to discuss. Use humour on "Miss World" questions if you have
any. For questions you cannot answer, be honest (*May I get back to you on this?*) and follow
up. If an interviewer looks unsatisfied, check (*Are there any parts you want me to elaborate or
clarify?*).

**Specific questions.** *What you learned in college* → focus on high-level methods and research
rather than specific courses; gear toward future courses. *Program and country of choice* → give
answers in order of priority: advances in your field (specific schools/professors) · solutions to
your own problems and lessons for Vietnam · education-system advantages · sources of inspiration
· cultural aspects including diversity · (cute) personal reasons. *Why should we choose you* →
relevant qualities · sufficient preparation · strong sense of purpose (*I want it the most*) ·
potential contributions to home country and program · something unique and funny → *I believe I'm
a great investment*. Beware the over-qualified-candidate case. *Application irregularities* →
show progress, demonstrate how you overcame it, put things in perspective **without putting down
others**. *Handle-a-hypothetical-problem* → treat as a **mini-proposal**: problem identification
→ possible challenges → solutions → action plan.

**Post-interview:** ask for business cards and send a thank-you note — gratitude for their time,
any additional information or follow-up, and (if unsuccessful) a request for constructive
feedback.

Question bank by topic: `S33`/`S38 materials/Interview — Sample questions.pdf`.

## Rules

- **Never fabricate** achievements, experiences, publications, scores, references or numbers.
  When a section needs content the user has not supplied, ask — do not invent a plausible story.
- The user's material is the content; the method supplies the structure and the prose.
- Apply the full annwuyn language pipeline to every draft, and economy of language especially —
  word limits are hard.
- Preserve the writer's voice. A technically cleaner PS that no longer sounds like them is worse.
- Run `references/11-vn-transfer.md` items 18–25 on every SOP and PS.
- Explanations in the user's language; the document itself in English.
