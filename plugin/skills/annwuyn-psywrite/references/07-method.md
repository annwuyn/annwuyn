# 07 — The Method section

Source: Beins & Beins (2021), ch. 11, pp. 131–144, with the APA reporting standards (JARS:
Appelbaum et al., 2018, for quantitative work; Levitt et al., 2018, for qualitative and mixed
methods; apastyle.apa.org/jars).

## Purpose and reader (pp. 131–132)

The Method links the abstract ideas of the Introduction to the concrete means used to study
them. It does two jobs: it lets readers judge how well the method answers the question and
supports the conclusions, and it gives other researchers what they need to replicate the study.

- Too little detail and readers cannot assess or replicate the study; too much and they get lost
  in facts that do not matter.
- APA lists 13 possible topics for a Method section; few papers need all of them. Use JARS as the
  full checklist.
- Write for a reader who is **intelligent but uninformed**: they know research, they do not know
  your study.

## Order of subsections

Participants (or Subjects) → Materials and/or Apparatus → Procedure → Design. Merge or reorder
when the study is clearer that way; the reader must be able to find the critical information
(p. 144). Level 2 headings (02).

## Participants (pp. 132–138)

**Terms.** “Participants” for people who know they are in a study; APA uses “subjects” for
non-human animals (p. 133).

**What to report (Table 11.1):**

| Characteristic | Report |
|---|---|
| Age (older children, adolescents, adults) | mean age in years; range and/or *SD* |
| Age (young children) | age in months; range and/or *SD* |
| Sex and gender | numbers of each; use participants’ own terms; nouns *woman*/*man*, adjectives *female*/*male* |
| Race and ethnicity | general designations, and subgroups when culture is a focus (for example, Vietnamese within Asian) |
| Recruitment | convenience, notices, purposive, chain-referral; or simple/stratified random sampling with the population named |
| Inducement | course credit, raffle, money (with amount), none |
| Completion | how many finished, how many did not |

**Match the detail to the question (Box 11.1, pp. 136–137).** A study of joke ratings needs only
numbers, sex, age range and credit. A study of friendship across ethnic groups needs ethnicity of
each pair. A cross-national workplace study needs jobs, hours, tenure and nationality. Report every
characteristic that bears on the hypotheses (for a visual memory study: age, education, visual
arts background, visual impairment).

**Categories need care.** Self-identified race or ethnicity shifts with context, and people within
one label can differ widely; comparisons across groups need explicit, consistent categories
(p. 135).

**Recruitment and completion shape results.** Clinic-referred samples can show more severe
symptoms than community samples; completers may differ from dropouts (pp. 135–136).

**Attrition (pp. 138–140).** Report it in the Method and discuss its possible effect in the
Discussion. If you can compare those who left with those who stayed, report that comparison.

**Confidentiality (p. 138).** Anonymous: no one can link data to a person. Confidential: no one
outside the project can. Give no detail that could identify a participant; take extra care with
sensitive topics.

**Non-human subjects (pp. 138–139, Box 11.2).** Genus and species, strain, supplier, age, sex,
weight, housing, light cycle, diet and water, handling, testing times, and prior experimental
history.

## Materials and apparatus (pp. 140–142)

- **Materials** are printed or on screen (questionnaires, stimulus words); **apparatus** is what
  the researcher uses to present stimuli or record data. The book’s test: drop an apparatus and it
  breaks; drop materials and they scatter.
- Name ordinary equipment (stopwatch, computer) without detail. Describe specialised equipment
  fully, with maker and model.
- Well-known instruments: name and cite. Instruments by others that readers may not know:
  describe and say where to obtain them. Instruments you built: describe in detail (stimuli can go
  in an appendix).
- For questionnaires and scales: number of items, subscales, response format, and reliability from
  earlier work (and from your sample, per APA 7).

## Procedure (pp. 142–143)

Give the sequence of steps, what the researcher did and what participants did, including:

- variables manipulated and measured (independent and dependent);
- conditions or groups compared;
- how participants were assigned to groups;
- the researcher’s role in the session;
- the directions participants received (a summary; give exact wording when the instructions are
  the manipulation);
- the activities participants carried out.

Test: could someone run the study in all its important respects from this text? If not, add
detail. Informed consent can be mentioned here to show ethical compliance, though it is not part
of data collection (p. 143).

## Design (pp. 143–144, Table 11.2)

- **Type:** experiment, quasi-experiment, mixed design, correlational, observational, archival.
- **Variables:** independent, dependent, matched, extraneous or confounding.
- **Assignment:** random, systematic, by pre-existing characteristic.
- Repeated measures or independent measures.

## Additions from APA 7 and JARS

Sample-size planning (power analysis or other rationale), exclusion rules decided before analysis,
preregistration if any, data and materials availability, ethics approval with committee name and
number, and data analysis plan. For qualitative studies: researcher description and reflexivity,
data sources, recruitment, analytic procedure and methodological integrity (Levitt et al., 2018).

## Checklist

1. Can an informed outsider replicate the study from this section?
2. Are participant characteristics matched to the hypotheses?
3. Recruitment, inducement, completion and attrition reported?
4. Every measure: items, format, scoring, reliability, source?
5. Design, variables and assignment stated in one place?
6. No identifying details about participants?
