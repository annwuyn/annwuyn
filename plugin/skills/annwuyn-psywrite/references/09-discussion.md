# 09 — The Discussion section

Source: Beins & Beins (2021), ch. 13, pp. 169–176, with ch. 8 (pp. 103–104) on interpretation.

## Purpose (pp. 169–170)

Method and Results stay inside your study. The Discussion opens a conversation: what the study
says about the topic, how it relates to other work, why it matters, and where the ideas lead. It
answers the Introduction’s questions from the other end: what you set out to do, what you found,
whether expectations held, and why psychologists should care.

## Order of moves

1. **Restate the findings in words (p. 170).** One paragraph or two per finding or group of related
   findings, most important first. No statistics; they are in the Results.
2. **Link each finding to its hypothesis (pp. 170–171).** A short statement is enough (Kaiser,
   Vick, & Major, 2006: the hypothesis, then “our findings were consistent with this hypothesis”).
   Take multiple hypotheses one at a time. Saying a hypothesis was not supported is normal;
   results can confirm a hypothesis fully, partly or not at all.
3. **Connect the parts (pp. 171–172).** With several dependent variables or studies, say what they
   show together that one alone would not. The authors’ humour studies found that mood did not
   change how funny people rated jokes but did change how much they laughed and smiled; two
   measures revealed what one hid. When measures disagree, report it, explain it if you can, and
   say so if you cannot.
4. **Deal with nonsignificant and puzzling results (p. 172).**
   - A nonsignificant result may mean no effect, random error, or a design problem. Low power is an
     explanation only with a strong argument (variable data, a real but weak effect).
   - A predicted null result can support your account; an unexpected null may leave you only able
     to say the result is puzzling.
   - A significant result with no sensible explanation may be a Type I error. Say so and suggest a
     study to check it; do not invent a convoluted story.
5. **Compare with earlier research (pp. 172–174).** Link findings to specific studies that agree
   (Reder et al., 2006) and explain differences: methods, samples, species, tasks (Wang, 2006). If
   you found no related research, you have not searched enough.
6. **State importance and implications (p. 174).** Show how the study advances knowledge:
   support for a theory, or limits on it. Connect to current work in the field; saying the
   question is important does not make it so. Point to the questions the study opens: everyday
   settings for a laboratory finding, other populations, other materials.
7. **Acknowledge limitations (pp. 174–176).**
   - Measures and conditions: other choices might give other results; there is more work to do.
     Do not belabour limitations shared by standard methods.
   - Sample: students, mostly women, one culture; and categories of sex and gender that may be
     incomplete.
   - Causality: correlational and quasi-experimental designs do not support causal claims, above
     all with intact groups and no random assignment.
   - Attrition, if any, and how it may have shaped the data (ch. 11).
8. **Close with the take-home point.** One or two sentences on what the reader should remember.

## Interpretation rules (ch. 8, p. 104)

Every data set allows more than one interpretation. Offer the most plausible one given your data
and earlier findings, name the main alternatives, and say what research would decide between them.

## Language

Hedge in proportion to the evidence (`annwuyn` reference `07`, modality and qualification):
“suggest”, “is consistent with”, “may” for correlational evidence; stronger verbs only for
replicated experimental evidence. Present tense for conclusions; past tense for what was found.

## Multi-study papers

Each experiment can have a short Discussion; a **General Discussion** at the end integrates all
studies (Box 15.2, p. 204).

## Checklist

1. First paragraph restates the main finding in words and answers the research question.
2. Every hypothesis gets a verdict.
3. Multiple measures or studies are integrated.
4. Nonsignificant and puzzling results are handled plainly.
5. At least the key studies from the Introduction come back, with reasons for any disagreement.
6. Implications tied to theory and to current work.
7. Limitations specific to this study, causal language matched to the design.
8. A closing take-home sentence.
