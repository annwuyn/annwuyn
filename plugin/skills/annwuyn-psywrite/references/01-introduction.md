# 01 — The Introduction

An Introduction does three jobs: it tells readers what the paper is about, why the question
matters, and how the study answers it. Heard (2016, ch. 10) traces these jobs to Cicero:
attract the reader, prepare the ground, preview the case. Since abstracts took over the preview,
setting context is the main work left to the Introduction.

---

## 1. The shape: an hourglass, read from the top

A research paper widens at both ends and narrows in the middle (Bem, 2004; Heard, 2016,
ch. 8). The Introduction is the top half of the hourglass. It starts broad enough for the
journal’s readers and narrows to one question, one design and a set of hypotheses.

**How broad to start depends on the journal.** A general journal needs a first sentence any
psychologist can follow. A specialised journal can open inside the topic. Heard (2016, p. 85)
shows the gap with two physics papers, one in *Nature* and one in a narrow journal. Ask for
the target journal before choosing an opening.

---

## 2. The moves (Swales’s CARS model)

Swales (1990; revised 2004) described research introductions as three moves. Feak and Swales
(2011) give a full teaching treatment.

| Move | What it does | Typical steps |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Establish a territory** | Shows the topic is worth a reader’s time | claim importance · make topic generalisations · review prior work |
| **2. Establish a niche** | Shows what is missing or wrong | counter-claim · indicate a gap · raise a question · continue a tradition |
| **3. Occupy the niche** | Shows what this study does about it | state purpose or questions · state hypotheses · outline the approach · announce main findings (optional) · state value · preview structure |

Points that matter in practice:

- **Move 2 takes several forms, and a “gap” is one of them.** Heard (2016, pp. 86–87) lists the
  options: a gap in knowledge, a contradiction in the literature, a published claim open to new
  evidence, rival models that new data can separate, and a new way of framing the area.
  “No study has examined X in Vietnam” is the weakest form. It needs a reason why the Vietnamese
  case would change the answer.
- **Moves cycle.** Long introductions repeat Move 1 → Move 2 several times, one cycle per
  construct, before Move 3.
- **Whether to announce findings is a field choice.** Heard (2016, p. 87) recommends stating the
  main result at the end of the Introduction. Psychology journals usually end on hypotheses.
  Follow the target journal.

## 3. Problem → gap → hook

Lingard (2015) compresses the moves into three questions a reader must accept:

1. **Problem.** What issue in the world are people already talking about?
2. **Gap.** What do we not yet know or understand about it?
3. **Hook.** Why does that gap matter? What goes wrong, or stays unclear, while it stays open?

A gap without a hook is the most common failure. Readers agree the gap exists and see no reason
to care. Grant and Pollock (2011) make the same point for management journals: after the hook,
an Introduction names the conversation the paper joins, where that conversation has not gone,
and why it should go there.

---

## 4. The opening repertoire

The first one to three sentences decide whether a reviewer reads with interest or with
suspicion. Kendall, Silk and Chu (2000) and Bem (2004) recommend opening with something readers
can grasp at once, then moving to the literature. Grant and Pollock (2011) found two archetypes
in award-winning articles: the **quote** (a provocative statement or a short story) and the
**trend** (a pattern in the world or in the literature).

The table extends these into a working repertoire. Examples are invented for illustration.

| # | Opening type | Use when | Risk | Example first sentence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **People first** (Bem, 2004) | The behaviour is familiar to everyone | Sounds chatty if stretched | “Most students have reread a chapter the night before an exam and felt they knew it, only to blank on the first question.” |
| 2 | **Striking fact or figure** | A credible number makes the stakes concrete | Number must be sourced and current | “[X]% of secondary students report symptoms of exam-related anxiety ([source], [year]).” |
| 3 | **Question** | The paper answers one sharp question | Rhetorical questions in a row feel like a lecture | “Why do people who know that sleep improves memory still stay up late before exams?” |
| 4 | **Puzzle or contradiction** | Findings disagree, or theory predicts the opposite of what happens | Must be a real contradiction, with citations on both sides | “Retrieval practice improves long-term memory in laboratory studies, yet in classrooms its effect is often small.” |
| 5 | **Trend in the world** | A recent change creates a new problem | Ages quickly; avoid hype | “Since 2020, most university courses in Vietnam have kept some online component.” |
| 6 | **Trend in the literature** | A field has moved and left something behind | Can read as a list of citations | “Research on academic burnout has shifted from teachers to students over the past two decades.” |
| 7 | **Short case or vignette** | One case captures the phenomenon | Needs to be brief; a case is no evidence | “A first-year student describes her online classes as ‘a room full of black squares.’” |
| 8 | **Historical turn** | The question has a known origin | Long histories lose readers | “Ebbinghaus’s forgetting curve, published in 1885, still frames how researchers think about review schedules.” |
| 9 | **Theoretical debate** | Two models make different predictions | Reader must know both models exist | “Two accounts explain the testing effect, and they predict different results for delayed tests.” |
| 10 | **Applied or policy stake** | The finding will change a decision | Overclaiming practical value | “Schools are deciding whether to ban phones in class with little evidence on what happens to attention.” |
| 11 | **Definition with a twist** | A familiar term is used loosely | A dictionary definition is dull | “‘Engagement’ appears in nearly every study of online learning and is measured in at least four different ways.” |
| 12 | **Direct statement** | Specialised journal, expert readers | Too abrupt for general journals | “This study tests whether retrieval practice reduces test anxiety in secondary school students.” |
| 13 | **Quote** | A sentence from a source says something no paraphrase can | Overused; quote must be short and earned | A one-line statement from a participant, a policy text or a classic author |

**Openings to avoid:** sweeping claims (“Since ancient times, humans have…”), dictionary
definitions, “Research has shown that…” with no content, a national policy document with no
link to the research question, and a first paragraph that only praises the topic’s importance.

**Workflow:** draft three openings of different types, keep the one that leads most directly
to the gap, and cut the rest.

### How the opening appears in a talk or a poster

- **Talk:** open with something interesting and memorable, then give two or three main points.
  Name individual researchers only when the audience knows them (Beins & Beins, 2021, Table 17.1,
  p. 239).
- **Poster:** the Introduction usually shrinks to a box titled with the basic questions and
  hypotheses (Beins & Beins, 2021, p. 227).

---

## 5. The middle: the literature as an argument

- **Organise by idea.** Each paragraph makes one claim about the literature and uses studies
  as evidence for it. A paragraph that walks through one study after another (“Smith found…
  Jones found… Lee found…”) leaves the reader to find the point.
- **Topic sentence = the claim.** Start each paragraph with what the studies show together,
  then cite them.
- **Lead every paragraph toward the gap.** If a paragraph would not be missed when deleted, it
  belongs in a review article.
- **Define constructs where they enter.** A construct used in a hypothesis needs a definition
  and a measure-relevant description before the hypothesis appears.
- **Tense.** Past tense for a completed study (“Gavanski noted”); present tense for a claim
  that still holds (“Group size affects mirth responses”). The sample manuscript is corrected
  for this (Beins & Beins, 2021, p. 268).
- **Voice and person.** Active voice. A sole author writes “I” (p. 268).
- **Plain wording.** Beins and Beins mark a sentence about “internal and affective
  psycho-emotional responses … as detected through the use of a research-generated self-report
  inventory” as gobbledygook (p. 268). Say what was measured.

---

## 6. The end: the present study and hypotheses

A psychology Introduction normally ends with:

1. one sentence that states the purpose and links it to the gap;
2. a short outline of the design (who, what was manipulated or measured, how);
3. hypotheses, each with its reason in the preceding text. Numbered hypotheses (H1, H2) help
   when the Results section tests them in order;
4. for multi-study papers, one line per study explaining why the next study was needed.

**Format:** APA 7 gives the Introduction no heading. The paper’s title, centred and bold, sits
at the top of the first text page and the Introduction starts below it (Beins & Beins, 2021,
Box 15.1, p. 203; the error is marked in Appendix A, p. 268). Method follows directly after the
Introduction, on the same page (p. 202).

## 7. Notes for Vietnamese writers

- Novice Vietnamese TESOL writers already use all three moves, adapted to local expectations
  (Nguyen & Pramoolsook, 2014). The usual problems are proportion and order: a long Move 1 built
  from national context, a short Move 2, and a Move 3 with no hypotheses.
- A Vietnamese reader accepts “this topic has not been studied in Vietnam” as a gap. An
  international reviewer asks why Vietnam would change the result. Supply that reason
  (a cultural, institutional or policy difference that theory says should matter).
- Policy openings (“Resolution 29-NQ/TW states…”) work only when the policy creates the
  problem the study addresses. Otherwise open with the people or the phenomenon.
- Run the `annwuyn` pipeline on the draft: linearity (`01`), thematic progression (`02`) and
  engagement (`06`) catch most problems in introductions written from Vietnamese drafts.
- For wording, use the Phrasebank section “Introducing work” (pp. 7–21) through
  `phrasebank.py`, after the move map is fixed.

## 8. Diagnostic checklist for an Introduction draft

1. Does the first paragraph name the phenomenon in terms a non-specialist reader can follow?
2. Can you underline the territory, the gap and the hook in separate sentences?
3. Is the gap one of the Move 2 forms, and does the hook say why it matters?
4. Does each paragraph open with a claim and use studies as evidence?
5. Is every construct in the hypotheses defined earlier in the text?
6. Does each hypothesis have a stated reason?
7. Is the opening breadth right for the target journal?
8. Tense, person and voice: past for completed studies, “I” for a sole author, active voice.
9. No “Introduction” heading; title repeated at the top of the text.
10. Read the first sentence of every paragraph in sequence. Do they tell the argument on their own?
