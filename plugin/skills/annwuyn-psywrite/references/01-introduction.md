# 01 — The Introduction

Main source: Beins & Beins (2021), ch. 10, pp. 121–130, extended with genre research and writing
guides (see `SOURCES.md`). Page numbers refer to the book unless another source is named.

---

## 0. The four questions (pp. 121–122)

Beins and Beins reduce the Introduction to four questions:

1. **Why is the topic interesting, and how will the paper advance knowledge?**
2. **What do we already know?**
3. **What are you doing?**
4. **What do you expect to happen, and why?**

Put as tasks: (a) identify the topic; (b) say what psychologists know and which questions remain
open; (c) explain how the study fills a gap; (d) state the hypotheses and the logic behind them;
(e) do all of this in clear, engaging prose. The hard part is enough detail for a good grasp of
the material and no detail that distracts.

At the end of the Introduction four things should be obvious: the topic, the current state of
knowledge, unambiguous expectations, and the potential impact of the study (p. 130).

---

## 1. The shape: an hourglass, read from the top

Broad, general ideas come first; details specific to the study come later (p. 122). A research
paper widens at both ends and narrows in the middle (Bem, 2004; Heard, 2016, ch. 8). The
Introduction is the top half: it starts broad enough for the journal’s readers and narrows to
one question, one design and a set of hypotheses.

**How broad to start depends on the journal.** A general journal needs a first sentence any
psychologist can follow; a specialised journal can open inside the topic (Heard, 2016, p. 85).
Ask for the target journal before choosing an opening.

## 2. The moves (Swales’s CARS model)

Swales (1990; revised 2004) described research introductions as three moves. Feak and Swales
(2011) give a full teaching treatment.

| Move | What it does | Typical steps |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Establish a territory** | Shows the topic is worth a reader’s time | claim importance · make topic generalisations · review prior work |
| **2. Establish a niche** | Shows what is missing or wrong | counter-claim · indicate a gap · raise a question · continue a tradition |
| **3. Occupy the niche** | Shows what this study does about it | state purpose or questions · state hypotheses · outline the approach · announce main findings (optional) · state value · preview structure |

- **Move 2 takes several forms, and a “gap” is one of them.** Beins and Beins (ch. 9,
  pp. 109–113) name three ways research frames a topic: filling a gap, building on a particular
  study (for example, testing a tool built on male samples with female patients), and exploring
  competing theories. Heard (2016, pp. 86–87) adds a published claim open to new evidence and a
  new way of framing the area. “No study has examined X in Vietnam” is the weakest form. It needs
  a reason why the Vietnamese case would change the answer.
- **Moves cycle.** Long introductions repeat Move 1 → Move 2 once per construct before Move 3.
- **Announcing findings is a field choice.** Heard (2016, p. 87) states the main result at the
  end of the Introduction; psychology journals usually end on hypotheses and implications.

## 3. Problem → gap → hook

Lingard (2015) compresses the moves into three questions a reader must accept:

1. **Problem.** What issue in the world are people already talking about?
2. **Gap.** What do we not yet know or understand about it?
3. **Hook.** Why does that gap matter? What goes wrong, or stays unclear, while it stays open?

A gap without a hook is the most common failure. Grant and Pollock (2011) make the same point:
after the hook, an Introduction names the conversation the paper joins, where that conversation
has not gone, and why it should go there.

## 4. The opening repertoire

### What Beins and Beins show (pp. 122–125)

The book compares three openings for a study of sense of humour (p. 122). The strong one makes a
surprising claim about people (nearly everyone believes their sense of humour is above average,
which cannot be true for everyone) and hints at the angle. The weak ones announce that many
studies exist, or that humour matters and so psychologists study it. Both name the topic and
give no reason to read on.

The book then lists approaches found in published articles, each with a real example:

| Approach | What it looks like | Book’s example (paraphrased) |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope, successes and limits of earlier work** (p. 123) | The plain, most common opening: state the topic and what earlier work achieved or failed to achieve | Turnwald et al. (2019): nutrition-information campaigns have had limited effect on what people eat |
| **An actual event** (p. 123) | A real incident that embodies the phenomenon | Kruger and Dunning (1999): a bank robber who believed lemon juice made his face invisible to cameras |
| **A fictional scenario** (pp. 123–124) | A short imagined situation the reader steps into | Molouki et al. (2019): a game-show contestant offered a steak dinner, then told to eat a cockroach |
| **A statistic** (p. 124) | One number that makes the stakes concrete; avoid dull numbers | Leading causes of death in a given year; the share of a population infected with HIV |
| **A quotation** (pp. 124–125) | A quote striking for its content, or for who said it | A 1930s teacher longing for a strongman leader (Sprong et al., 2019); Freud on repression (Boag, 2006) |
| **A common occurrence** (p. 125) | An experience readers recognise | Hiring discrimination in jobs typed as male or female (Uhlmann & Cohen, 2005) |

Two rules from the same pages:

- **Verify every quotation at its source.** Quote sites misattribute freely (p. 125).
- **Open with the idea.** Readers do not care, in a first sentence, about author names they do not
  know or the year of publication (“In 1999, Dunning and Kruger found…”). Ask whether you would
  enjoy reading your own opening (p. 125). Bem (2004) gives the same advice: open with people and
  their behaviour.

### The extended repertoire

Kendall, Silk and Chu (2000), whom Beins and Beins cite, list further ways to begin. Grant and
Pollock (2011) found two archetypes in award-winning articles: the **quote** (a provocative
statement or a short story) and the **trend** (a pattern in the world or in the literature).
Combined with the book’s list, this gives a working repertoire. Examples are invented.

| # | Opening type | Use when | Risk | Example first sentence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Common occurrence / people first** (pp. 122, 125; Bem, 2004) | The behaviour is familiar to everyone | Sounds chatty if stretched | “Most students have reread a chapter the night before an exam and felt they knew it, only to blank on the first question.” |
| 2 | **Statistic** (p. 124) | A credible number makes the stakes concrete | Must be sourced, current, and interesting | “[X]% of secondary students report symptoms of exam-related anxiety ([source], [year]).” |
| 3 | **Actual event** (p. 123) | A real case embodies the phenomenon | Needs a source; one case is no evidence | A documented incident, two sentences, then the general claim |
| 4 | **Fictional scenario** (pp. 123–124) | The reader can step into a decision | Silly if the scenario is far from the study | “Imagine you are offered a free meal on one condition…” |
| 5 | **Quotation** (pp. 124–125) | The wording or the speaker matters | Misattribution; overuse | A short, verified line from a participant, a historical text or a classic author |
| 6 | **Scope and limits of earlier work** (p. 123) | Specialised journal, expert readers | Flat if the limit is not stated | “Information campaigns have done little to change how students study.” |
| 7 | **Question** | The paper answers one sharp question | A run of rhetorical questions feels like a lecture | “Why do people who know that sleep improves memory still stay up late before exams?” |
| 8 | **Puzzle or contradiction** | Findings disagree, or theory predicts the opposite of what happens | Needs citations on both sides | “Retrieval practice improves long-term memory in laboratory studies, yet in classrooms its effect is often small.” |
| 9 | **Trend in the world** | A recent change creates a new problem | Ages quickly; avoid hype | “Since 2020, most university courses in Vietnam have kept some online component.” |
| 10 | **Trend in the literature** | A field has moved and left something behind | Can read as a list of citations | “Research on academic burnout has shifted from teachers to students over the past two decades.” |
| 11 | **Theoretical debate** | Two models make different predictions | Reader must know both models exist | “Two accounts explain the testing effect, and they predict different results for delayed tests.” |
| 12 | **Applied or policy stake** | The finding will change a decision | Overclaiming practical value | “Schools are deciding whether to ban phones in class with little evidence on what happens to attention.” |
| 13 | **Definition with a twist** | A familiar term is used loosely | A dictionary definition is dull | “‘Engagement’ appears in nearly every study of online learning and is measured in at least four different ways.” |

**Openings to avoid:** sweeping claims (“Since ancient times…”), dictionary definitions,
“Research has shown that…” with no content, “many studies have examined X”, author-and-year
first sentences, and a national policy document with no link to the research question.

**Workflow:** draft three openings of different types, keep the one that leads most directly to
the gap, and cut the rest.

### In a talk or a poster

- **Talk:** open with something interesting and memorable, then name two or three main points;
  name researchers only when the audience knows them (Table 17.1, p. 239).
- **Poster:** the Introduction shrinks to a box of basic questions and hypotheses (p. 227).

## 5. The middle: the literature as an argument (pp. 126–128)

- **Start broad, with little detail.** The first part of an Introduction cites work for the overall
  question, without study-level detail. Russano et al. (2005) covered five broad ideas about
  confessions in three paragraphs and eleven references (p. 126).
- **Show how ideas connect and where they are going.** A list of one study after another reads as
  unrelated facts (pp. 126–127). Open each paragraph with the claim the studies support together.
- **Illustrative coverage is enough.** Read enough to give a reasonably complete account; choose
  representative examples (p. 126).
- **Present the controversies.** Milgram’s obedience studies are often taught as settled; there is
  real dispute over the method and its interpretation, and an Introduction on obedience should
  present it (p. 127).
- **Data and theory, no opinions.** Your opinion is not the content of an Introduction (p. 127).
- **Why review at all (p. 127):** to avoid repeating a study already done; to show where your
  idea fits (and show expertise); to spot limitations you can remedy; to borrow methods,
  materials and analyses, with credit.
- **Method detail** belongs in the Introduction only when it shapes the study’s logic (p. 128).
- **Clarify terms (p. 128).** Define terms that mean something else to lay readers
  (“schizophrenia”) or to other subfields (“SSRI” is a drug class and also a self-report
  inventory). Discuss operational definitions when measurement is disputed or you propose a
  better one.
- **Tense, person, voice.** Past tense for completed studies; present for claims that still hold;
  a sole author writes “I”; active voice (Appendix A, p. 268).
- **Plain wording.** Say what was measured (Appendix A marks a string of abstract nouns as
  gobbledygook, p. 268).

## 6. The end: hypotheses and implications (pp. 129–130)

**Build the hypothesis from an idea chain.** A hypothesis carries weight when it follows from
existing theory and findings; hunches carry no weight because different people form different hunches from the same ideas (p. 130). The book’s example (Goldenberg et al., 1999) links
four published ideas (neurotic people are conscious of mortality; neuroticism goes with discomfort
about sex; thoughts of sex link to thoughts of death; romantic framing breaks that link) into one
prediction, shown as a diagram (Box 10.2): Idea 1 + Idea 2 → hypothesis.

Write the chain as:

```
Idea 1 (source) → Idea 2 (source) → [Idea 3 (source)] → Hypothesis (if … then …)
```

Then in the text:

1. one sentence that states the purpose and links it to the gap;
2. a short outline of the design (who, what was manipulated or measured, how);
3. hypotheses, each with its reason in the preceding text; number them (H1, H2) when the
   Results test them in order;
4. **implications:** what it will mean for the theory if the data support the hypothesis (p. 130);
5. for multi-study papers, one line per study on why the next study was needed.

**Format:** APA 7 gives the Introduction no heading. The title, centred and bold, sits at the top
of the first text page and the Introduction starts below it (Box 15.1, p. 203; Appendix A,
p. 268). Method follows on the same page (p. 202).

## 7. Notes for Vietnamese writers

- Novice Vietnamese TESOL writers already use all three moves, adapted to local expectations
  (Nguyen & Pramoolsook, 2014). Common problems are proportion and order: a long Move 1 built
  from national context, a short Move 2, and a Move 3 with no hypotheses.
- A Vietnamese reader accepts “this has not been studied in Vietnam” as a gap. An international
  reviewer asks why Vietnam would change the result. Supply that reason (a cultural,
  institutional or policy difference that theory says should matter).
- Policy openings (“Resolution 29-NQ/TW states…”) work only when the policy creates the problem
  the study addresses. Otherwise use one of the openings in §4.
- Run the `annwuyn` pipeline on the draft: linearity (`01`), thematic progression (`02`) and
  engagement (`06`) catch most problems in introductions written from Vietnamese drafts.
- For wording, use the Phrasebank section “Introducing work” (pp. 7–21) through
  `phrasebank.py`, after the move map is fixed.

## 8. Diagnostic checklist for an Introduction draft

1. Can a reader answer the four questions in §0 from the text alone?
2. Is the opening one of the types in §4, and would you enjoy reading it?
3. Can you underline the territory, the gap and the hook in separate sentences?
4. Does each paragraph open with a claim and use studies as evidence?
5. Are the main controversies in the area presented?
6. Is every construct in the hypotheses defined, with its operational definition if disputed?
7. Does each hypothesis follow from an idea chain with sources?
8. Are the implications of a confirmed hypothesis stated?
9. Is the opening breadth right for the target journal?
10. Tense, person and voice; no “Introduction” heading; title repeated on the first text page.
11. Read the first sentence of every paragraph in sequence. Do they tell the argument alone?
