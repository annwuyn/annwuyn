# 11 — From topic to sources: ideas, searching, reading and integrity

Source: Beins & Beins (2021), Part I: ch. 2 “Formulating Your Ideas”, ch. 3 on assessing sources,
ch. 4 “How to Conduct a Literature Search”, ch. 5 “How to Read and Summarize a Journal Article”
(pp. 13–62). Page references are given at chapter level where the photographs carry no page
numbers.

## Finding the focal question (ch. 2)

- Start from course readings and the instructor; skim scholarly and popular sources in a
  pre-research phase to find what interests you.
- Read before fixing a thesis. Research gives you the thesis, and it protects you from repeating
  published work or missing studies that undercut it (ch. 9, p. 108).
- Narrow until the question can be answered within the paper’s limits, and ask why an academic
  audience would care.
- Research, focus, thesis and writing feed each other; expect to move back and forth.
- Recognise multiple viewpoints. Psychologists bound their conclusions to specific populations and
  conditions because behaviour has many causes; read for those boundaries.

## Assessing sources (ch. 3)

**Popular versus scholarly.** Ask: who is the author (a researcher reporting their own work?),
who is the audience, is there peer review, are sources cited, how much method and data detail is
given? Popular articles simplify and omit, so they carry less weight for professionals.

**Read every source critically,** including journal articles: authors have biases and
assumptions, and every study has limits.

**Logical fallacies** to watch for in sources and in your own argument (appeal to authority,
appeal to what “everybody knows”, hasty generalisation, faulty cause and effect, and others; the
chapter tabulates them). See also `annwuyn` reference `09` (argument and fallacies).

**Internet sources:** check authority, objectivity (who runs the site and why), currency, and
the evidence behind claims. The chapter works through sites with commercial or advocacy purposes
and lists each site’s purpose and limitations.

## Searching the literature (ch. 4, pp. 41–51)

- Library catalogue for books; article databases for journal work. PsycINFO and PsycARTICLES are
  the main psychology databases; Web of Science / Social Sciences Citation Index shows who cited a
  work; Academic Search Premier is interdisciplinary; MEDLINE/PubMed for medical topics.
- Search strategy: key terms and synonyms; the database thesaurus (subject terms); Boolean
  operators; limits by date, population, language, document type.
- Search engines and Google Scholar find leads; verify through the database or publisher.
- **Use sources to find sources:** mine the reference lists of good articles (backward), and
  citation indexes for later work that cites them (forward).
- For a systematic review, use `academic-research-skills:deep-research` and `sr-screener`.

## Reading a journal article (ch. 5, pp. 51–62)

Read slowly and more than once; take notes section by section.

| Section | Questions to answer |
|---|---|
| Introduction | What is the issue? What have others found? What are the hypotheses? |
| Method | Who took part? What apparatus and materials? What did participants do? What was the design? |
| Results | What happened? Which statistical tests? Are the results significant, and how large? |
| Discussion | Were hypotheses supported? What do the authors conclude, and do the data justify it? What are the limitations? |
| References | Where did the ideas come from? Which sources should you read next? |

Write a short summary in your own words for each article (question, method, main result,
conclusion, limitation) before you use it in a paper.

## Ethical writing and plagiarism (ch. 2)

Plagiarism is presenting another person’s work as your own, intentionally or not. It includes:

- summarising someone’s idea without credit;
- using someone’s language without credit;
- using someone’s language without quotation marks, even with a citation;
- submitting work someone else wrote, published or not.

Common knowledge needs no citation, and what counts as common knowledge depends on the audience.

**Prevention starts with notes.** Record the source of every note; mark copied wording with
quotation marks at once. The chapter compares a source passage on self-determination theory
with a failed paraphrase (same sentence structure, words swapped, no citation) and a correct
rewrite (new structure, own words, citation).

**Quote, paraphrase or summarise (ch. 6).** Psychology prefers paraphrase and summary; quote only
when the exact wording matters. A string of citations for a broad claim gives indirect support and
needs no summary of each study.

Generative AI: follow the target journal’s and university’s policy; disclose use as required
(`/ars-disclosure`); never let a tool invent sources.
