# 06 — Ethics committee (IRB) proposals

Source: Beins & Beins (2021), ch. 19, pp. 255–263 (pp. 264 onward not yet photographed). The
chapter uses a proposal approved at Ithaca College for a humour study (Wimer & Beins, 2008) as
its running example.

## Why the committee exists (pp. 255–257)

Past research harmed participants. Laws now require review before data collection. The Belmont
Report (1979) names the three principles: respect for persons, beneficence and justice.

- Research with people goes to an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or human-subjects committee;
  research with animals to an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).
- The committee assesses risks and how you will keep them to a minimum; weighs benefits and new
  knowledge against risk; checks that participants get a fair description of risks; and checks
  privacy, anonymity and confidentiality.
- Institutions add their own rules: some require a faculty supervisor for student research, or
  completed ethics training before submission.
- Committees are not known for flexibility. Use their form and give exactly what they ask for.

Outside the United States the same review goes by other names (ethics committee, research
ethics board). In Vietnam and in European universities, check the faculty’s own procedure and
the national rules that apply to the study.

## Sections of the proposal

**Abstract (pp. 257–258).** A brief statement of what you intend to study, what materials you
will use and what participants will do. State the purpose. When risk is above minimal, the
committee must judge whether benefits outweigh it, so the purpose matters. This abstract differs
from a paper’s: it describes a study you plan to run and focuses on method.

**What participants will actually do (p. 258).** Give more detail than less. Questions the
committee cannot answer from the text delay approval. Include the full procedure, the stimuli,
any deception, and the debriefing.

**Risks and how you will deal with them (pp. 259–260).**
- Risk can be physical, psychological, social or economic; the last two are rare in
  psychology.
- Minimal risk: participants are no more likely to be harmed than in everyday life.
- Physical risk: name the behaviour that could cause injury and how you will reduce it.
- Psychological risk is more common: frustrating, boring or depressing tasks; false feedback
  that lowers self-esteem. Say why any effect will be minor and short-lived, and what you will do
  if a participant reacts badly (for example, debriefing that the task was hard for everyone).
  Make that promise only if you will keep it.

**Risk–benefit analysis (p. 260).** One study has modest benefit in itself; its value lies in
adding to a body of knowledge. Participants may learn how research works, gain some insight
into themselves and feel good about contributing. The welfare of participants comes first.

**Benefits of the study (p. 260).** State the benefits to participants concretely.

**Informed consent (pp. 260–261, Figure 19.1).** Tell participants in advance what they will
do and what risks they face; that participation is voluntary and they may withdraw at any time;
how data stay confidential and, where possible, anonymous. The sample form has seven parts:
purpose; benefits; what you will be asked to do; what to expect; more information and contacts;
withdrawal; confidentiality. It ends with a statement of agreement and age, signature, printed
name and date.

**Deception (p. 261).** Active deception must be declared to the committee. Passive deception
(withholding the hypothesis) is subtler: the question is whether the withheld information would
change a person’s decision to take part. Risks can never be withheld.

**Animal research (pp. 262–263).** Proposals to an IACUC justify the species and numbers,
describe procedures that may cause pain and how it will be relieved, and state housing, care
and euthanasia plans. The book summarises the federal principles, among them: avoid or minimise
discomfort, use sedation or anaesthesia for painful procedures, and have qualified people do
the work.
