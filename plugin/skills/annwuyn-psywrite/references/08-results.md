# 08 — The Results section and communicating statistics

Sources: Beins & Beins (2021), ch. 8 “Communicating Statistics” (pp. 95–105) and ch. 12 “The
Results Section” (pp. 145–167); APA *Publication Manual* (7th ed.), ch. 6–7; JARS-Quant
(Appelbaum et al., 2018).

## The principle: words carry the idea, numbers support it

- The hypothesis is the plot; the results are the surprises (p. 145). Ideas come first; statistics
  are a tool that supports them (pp. 146–147).
- Plan top-down with an outline or idea map. A list of statistics one after another has the
  interest of a phone book (p. 146).
- **Write each result in words first, then insert the numbers.** A reader should get the point
  without the statistics (pp. 146–147; Table 8.1, p. 100). Klinesmith et al. (2006) state in one
  sentence that handling a gun raised testosterone more than handling a toy, then add means,
  *SD*s, *t* and *d*.
- For each statistic give: a verbal statement of the point; the descriptive statistics; the
  inferential test; a verbal statement of what the numbers mean (ch. 8, pp. 98–99).

## Order and selection

- Report hypotheses in the order the Introduction stated them (p. 146).
- Start each result with descriptive statistics (means and *SD*s, or frequencies), then the
  inferential test (p. 147).
- Lead with the most important results; give as much detail as readers need, no more (p. 147).
- Do not explain standard tests to a research readership (“a *t* test compares two means”) or name
  common software beyond citing it (pp. 147–148).

## Significant and nonsignificant results (pp. 148–151)

- Report results that bear on the hypotheses whether significant or not.
- An unexpected significant result can be mentioned; do not build a convoluted explanation for it.
- **No p-hacking or selective reporting:** no peeking at significance mid-study and stopping when
  it appears; no reporting only the variables that worked. Both are unethical and have ended
  careers (p. 149).
- Write “nonsignificant”; “insignificant” means unimportant (p. 149).
- Report effect sizes and confidence intervals with every test, alongside exact *p* values
  (Wilkinson & Task Force, 1999; pp. 149–150).
- The .05 cut-off is convention. Report exact values (*p* = .06) and let readers judge; if you
  discuss a near-significant effect, say you do so with caution. Avoid “marginally significant”
  as a claim of support (pp. 150–151).

## APA format for statistics (pp. 150–153, Tables 12.2–12.4)

```
t(60) = 3.23, p = .002, d = 0.82, 95% CI [0.27, 1.15]
F(2, 33) = 5.25, p = .010, MSE = 2.95, η² = .24
r(186) = .25, p < .001, 95% CI [.11, .38]
χ²(2, N = 120) = 8.40, p = .015, V = .19
```

- Exact *p* values to two or three decimals; *p* < .001 when smaller (never *p* = .000).
- Test statistics to two decimals. Spaces around =, <, >.
- Italicise statistical symbols written in Latin letters (*M*, *SD*, *t*, *F*, *p*, *r*, *N*, *n*);
  Greek letters stay upright (α, β, η², χ²).
- For χ², give degrees of freedom and sample size in the parentheses.
- Standard abbreviations and symbols are tabulated in the book (Tables 12.3–12.4) and in the
  *Publication Manual*.

## Tables (pp. 154–158, Figures 12.1–12.3, Table 12.5)

- Use a table when the text would become a string of numbers readers cannot compare
  (p. 154). Two or three groups usually fit in a sentence.
- Layout: **Table 1** in bold on line one; the title in *Italic Title Case* on line two; horizontal
  rules only (under the title row, under column heads, at the bottom); no vertical lines; means
  with *SD*s in parentheses; marginal means if useful.
- Notes below the bottom rule, in this order: general note, specific notes, probability notes.
  Subscript letters can mark which means differ.
- Correlation tables: variables down the side and numbered across the top; lower triangle only.

## Figures (pp. 158–163, Figures 12.4–12.10)

- Line graph when the X-axis variable is quantitative; bar chart when it is categorical; scatter
  plot for correlations; histogram for frequencies (bars touch for a continuous variable and stand
  apart for categories).
- Label axes; put the legend inside the plot area; caption explains anything needed to read the
  figure, including what error bars show.
- **Honest scales.** A Y-axis that starts above zero, or a stretched Y-axis, inflates small
  differences (pp. 160–161). The figure must match the accurate message.
- Keep graphs clean: drop gridlines, moiré hatching and other chartjunk; maximise the data-to-ink
  ratio (Tufte, 1983; ch. 8, p. 102).

## Text and visuals together (pp. 164–165)

- Refer to every table and figure in the text and say what to see in it.
- Text and visuals complement each other; do not repeat the same numbers in both.
- Ask whether the table or figure makes the reader’s work easier. If words do the job, cut it.

## Results versus Discussion (p. 165)

The Results report data and statistics without interpretation: what happened, never why. A
statement in the Results should be one no reader could dispute from the data. Interpretation,
speculation, theory and links to other studies go in the Discussion.

## Wording numbers honestly (ch. 8, pp. 102–104)

- Make the claim match the data: “the number doubled since 1950” and “the number doubled every
  year since 1950” are different claims (Best, 2001).
- Give context: a dramatic case says nothing about a trend the data contradict; a comparison with
  a baseline when the problem did not exist misleads.
- “Significant” means reliable; with large samples, trivial differences are significant. Effect
  sizes tell readers whether a difference matters (p. 101).
- For lay audiences, explain what a mean is and that nobody may score exactly at it (p. 98).

## Checklist

1. Each hypothesis has a result paragraph, in Introduction order.
2. Each result: words → descriptives → test → effect size and CI → words.
3. All planned analyses reported, including nonsignificant ones; exploratory analyses labelled.
4. Exact *p*; no *p* = .000; symbols italicised correctly.
5. Tables: bold number, italic title, no vertical lines, notes in order.
6. Figures: right type for the variable, honest axes, caption explains error bars.
7. No interpretation in the Results.
