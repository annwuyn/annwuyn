# 05 — Oral presentations

Source: Beins & Beins (2021), ch. 17, pp. 237–246, with
Alley (2013) on slide design.

## Spoken English is a different register (pp. 237–238)

A listener cannot reread. The book shows one idea twice: a plain spoken version a listener can
follow, and the dense, citation-packed opening of a published article that works on the page and
fails aloud. In a talk:

- keep sentences short, so neither you nor the listener gets lost mid-sentence;
- choose which ideas to present and which to drop. Including everything overloads listeners;
  dropping key details leaves them unsure why you did the study, how, and what it means;
- lead listeners along a planned path and make each link between ideas explicit;
- repeat the key points.

## Adapting APA structure (p. 242)

Keep the paper’s order: introduction, method, results, discussion. A talk needs no abstract.
Cut most references. Few are needed to follow a talk, and names the audience has never heard do
nothing for them. Two exceptions: an expert audience will expect you to discuss the work you
build on; and if the talk covers your own series of studies, show the sequence.

## Guidelines by section (Table 17.1, pp. 239–240)

**Introduction**
- Open with something interesting and memorable that makes the audience care, then name your
  two or three main points.
- Describe earlier findings only as far as they led to your study.
- Name individual researchers only when the audience knows them.
- State the hypotheses and show how they follow from earlier work.
- Restate the logic briefly. Keep words on slides to a minimum and use graphics to carry the
  main points. Use a laser pointer sparingly.

**Method**
- Give the least information needed to understand the participants, materials and procedure.
- Present less detail than in a paper; a graphic can outline the materials.

**Results**
- Use graphics and plain English; describe statistics briefly.
- Keep tables and figures simple and give each result its own slide.
- Repeat results that matter later in the talk.

**Discussion**
- State briefly in plain English what the results mean and whether they support the hypotheses.
- End with a strong one-line summary.

**Questions**
- If you do not know, say so. If you know part, say what you know.
- Ask the questioner to rephrase if needed; repeat each question for the room.
- Pause before answering. Ask “Why might this be important?” to buy time.
- Do not let one person dominate; offer to talk afterwards.

**After the talk**
- Thank the audience. Collect your materials. Expect a dip in mood afterwards; it passes.

## Preparing (pp. 242–245, Table 17.2)

- Identify the two or three ideas listeners must leave with. Keep the background that leads to
  them, and drop the rest.
- Budget time by section. For a 15-minute slot: introduction 2 min, method 3, results 4,
  discussion 3, questions 3.
- Rehearse aloud and time it. Ask a friend to signal when you speak too fast.
- Know the equipment. Bring a backup: a handout or a copy of the slides on a second device.

## Delivery (pp. 245–246)

- Nerves are normal. Breathe; finish a sentence, pause, then go on. Keep water at hand.
- Speak; do not read notes or slides. Face the audience.
- Avoid filler words (“um”, “like”, “you know”).
- If you stumble, pause and restart the sentence smoothly. Listeners rarely notice slips the
  speaker does not point out.
- Nobody knows everything. Prepare well, and accept that “I don’t know” will sometimes happen.
- Make eye contact; imagine talking to a friend in the room.

## Slide design (reading list)

Alley’s (2013) assertion–evidence design: each slide headline is a full-sentence claim, and the
body is visual evidence for it (a graph, a photo, a diagram) with no bullet list.
