# 12 — Organising, revising and style

Source: Beins & Beins (2021), ch. 1 “Writing Professionally” (pp. 1–10), ch. 6 on organising a
paper, ch. 7 “Elements of Style”, ch. 9 (pp. 107–120) on argument. For sentence-level work, run
the `annwuyn` pipeline; this file adds the book’s rules.

## Professional writing (ch. 1)

- Scientific prose aims at precision: say what you mean and mean what you say, so the reader does
  not have to puzzle out the meaning (pp. 2–3).
- The goal is prose the reader does not notice, so the content comes first. Concision without
  omitting important information is the hard part.
- Technical terms carry complex ideas in few words; define them for readers who may not know them
  (pp. 3–4).
- Psychology persuades with logical argument from good data; appeals to authority or to what
  “everybody knows” do not count as evidence (pp. 5–6).
- Readers who see APA style ignored may assume the ideas were handled as carelessly (pp. 4–5).
- Writing happens only when you do it: fixed time, quiet place, a goal per session, a reward when
  it is reached (p. 9).

## Organising a paper (ch. 6; ch. 9, pp. 112–114)

- **Outlines and idea maps** at several scales: whole paper (thesis + main ideas), section,
  single paragraph. One idea per index card, with its citation; sort the cards into themes.
- **Paragraph test (ch. 9, p. 114).** For each paragraph: What is its main idea? How does it help
  answer the question? How does it strengthen the argument? Does it need more evidence? Does it
  contain evidence that does not fit? A paragraph that fails needs a new topic sentence, new
  evidence, or a new place.
- **Reverse outline** a draft: list each paragraph’s main idea; rewrite topic sentences that do not
  reflect the paragraph.
- Each section has its own focus inside the global focus (in a Method, ask how each paragraph
  explains how and why a method was chosen).

## Argument (ch. 9, pp. 114–120)

- An academic argument is the idea you want readers to accept as credible and valid, supported by
  evidence and sound logic.
- Premises must be accurate and complete; a valid-looking syllogism with a loose premise reaches a
  false conclusion.
- **Counterarguments (pp. 114–117):** refute those that are not credible; concede those that are,
  then show why your argument still holds or why the point lies outside your scope. Vrangalova and
  Savin-Williams (2011) explain a discrepancy with earlier work and cite studies that support their
  reading (Figure 9.1).
- **Strengthening logic (Table 9.4, pp. 119–120):** avoid “always / all / never” claims and claims
  about whole groups; be specific about populations; treat every study as limited, one study as no
  proof, and contradicting studies as both partly informative; support claims with evidence, never
  opinion; acknowledge counterarguments.
- Different perspectives make you more credible, close gaps in your argument, and expose biases
  (Table 9.3).

## Revising and editing (ch. 6)

- Revise for content and structure first; leave grammar and spelling to the end.
- Ask someone less familiar with the topic to point out where evidence or logic is thin.
- Read aloud, or have someone read to you; silent reading skips errors because you read what you
  meant. Punctuation changes meaning (“Eats, shoots and leaves”).

## Style (ch. 7)

**Audience and wording.** Match vocabulary and register to the readers; vary sentence length and
openings so the text does not plod. Use transitions for their function (add, contrast, show
similarity or exception, emphasise, summarise), and only where the link needs signalling (`annwuyn`
references `03` and `05`).

**Verbs.** Active voice. Cut weak forms of “to be” where an action verb fits (“We examine
beliefs…” for “It is beliefs that we examine…”). Subject–verb agreement, including singular
“they” with plural verb forms.

**Pronouns.** Clear antecedents; agreement in number; singular “they” for a person whose gender is
unknown or irrelevant, which removes “his/her”.

**Inclusive and bias-free language (APA 7, ch. 5).** Describe people at the level of detail the
study needs; use the terms groups use for themselves; person-first or identity-first language as
the community prefers; capitalise racial and ethnic groups (Black, White, Asian); “sexual
orientation” for the older “sexual preference”; age ranges in place of “the elderly”.

**Contractions and apostrophes.** APA discourages contractions. Its/it’s, their/they’re/there,
whose/who’s; plurals take no apostrophe (CEOs, 1990s); joint possession takes one apostrophe
(Thomas and Blackmun’s opinion), separate possession two (Thomas’s and Blackmun’s opinions).

**Specific words (APA).**

| Use | Note |
|---|---|
| data are, criteria are, phenomena are | plural nouns |
| hypothesis → hypotheses; analysis → analyses | |
| schemas, stigmas, indexes | modern plurals accepted |
| statistics (the discipline) is; statistics (numbers) are | |
| “while” and “since” | only in their time sense; use “although”, “whereas”, “because” otherwise |
| “that” / “which” | restrictive / non-restrictive (with commas) |
| “participants” | for people; “subjects” for animals |
| “nonsignificant” | for statistics; “insignificant” means unimportant |

## Checklist

1. Each paragraph passes the five-question test.
2. Counterarguments refuted or conceded.
3. No sweeping claims about whole groups.
4. Active verbs; clear pronouns; singular “they” where needed.
5. Bias-free terms; group labels as groups use them.
6. Read aloud once before submission.
