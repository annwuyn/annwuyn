# 03 — Common-error audit

Beins & Beins (2021) print a short two-experiment manuscript with its errors marked in callouts
(Appendix A, pp. 265–272), followed by the corrected version (Appendix B, pp. 273–). The list
below groups those marked errors into an audit, in the order a reader meets them. Use it as the
last pass over a full draft. Report each hit as: line → rule → fix.

## A. Pages and layout

1. Running head (if required) and page number at the top of every page, inserted through the
   header function (p. 267).
2. First text page repeats the title only. No author names or affiliation there (p. 268).
3. No heading “Introduction”. The text starts on the line under the title (p. 268).
4. No extra blank line before a new section (p. 269).
5. “Method” centred and bold; “Participants”, “Materials”, “Procedure” flush left and bold
   (p. 269).
6. “References” centred and bold, on a new page (p. 272).
7. A short, simple paper may combine Results and Discussion under one heading (p. 270).

## B. Abstract

8. “Abstract” centred; the paragraph is not indented (p. 267).

## C. Sentences

9. Active voice. “Participants’ reactions were examined” → “I examined participants’
   reactions” (pp. 267–268).
10. A sole author writes “I” and “my” (p. 268).
11. Past tense for work already completed: “Nosanchuk and Lightstone (1974) noted…” (p. 268).
12. Say it plainly. Strings of abstract nouns that hide what was measured are marked as
    “gobbledygook” twice (pp. 268, 271).
13. Cut empty phrases: “participants were instructed to rate” → “participants rated” (p. 269).
14. “Where” refers to place. Use “in which” for conditions and situations (p. 267).
15. Avoid “his/her”. Use singular “they/their” (p. 267).
16. Check for dangling wording: “When being debriefed, the manipulation…” reads as if the
    manipulation was debriefed. Make the participants the subject (p. 270).
17. No italics for emphasis. Build emphasis into the sentence (p. 271).
18. “Data” is plural: “the data show” (p. 270).

## D. Numbers and statistics

19. Do not begin a sentence with a numeral. Rewrite the sentence or write the number in words
    (p. 269).
20. Write out numbers below 10 in text (p. 269).
21. Italicise statistical symbols (*F*, *p*); give degrees of freedom: *F*(2, 114) = 9.88;
    spaces either side of “=” (p. 270).
22. Never report *p* = .000. Write *p* < .001 (p. 270).
23. Report an effect size with every test (p. 270).
24. Do not capitalise names of conditions or variables in running text (p. 270).
25. Capitalise “Experiment” when a number follows: “Experiment 2” (p. 269).
26. Italicise the anchors of a rating scale: 1 (*not funny*) to 7 (*very funny*) (p. 269).
27. Items in a series inside a sentence take (a), (b), (c) (p. 269).

## E. Participants and materials

28. Describe participants fully: number, age, gender, other characteristics that bear on the
    results, and how they were recruited (p. 269).

## F. Citations and references

29. “&” inside parentheses; “and” in running text (p. 268, p. 270).
30. Every source cited in the text appears in the reference list, and the reverse (p. 269).
31. Article titles in sentence case; journal names in title case and italics (p. 272).
32. Include the DOI as a URL when one exists (p. 272).

## G. Tables

33. “Table 1” in bold on its own line; the title below in italic title case (p. 271).
34. No vertical rules in tables (p. 271).

## Checks the appendix implies but does not mark

Beyond the callouts, check what the APA 7 *Publication Manual* and the reporting standards
(JARS: Appelbaum et al., 2018; Levitt et al., 2018) expect: sample-size rationale, exclusion
criteria, exact *p* values, confidence intervals, and data availability. The
`academic-research-skills` plugin (`/ars-citation-check`) checks references against sources.
