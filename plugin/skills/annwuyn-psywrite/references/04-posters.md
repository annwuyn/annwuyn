# 04 — Conference posters

Source: Beins & Beins (2021), ch. 16, pp. 221–235, with later poster design work from the
reading list (Faulkes, 2021; Morrison, 2019).

## How a poster differs from a paper (p. 224)

A reader of a paper can go back, check details and take their time. A visitor at a poster
session walks along a row, glances at titles and layouts, and stops only if the main points are
easy to find. A poster crowded with detail loses that visitor. Decide the two or three points you
want people to take away and present those simply and accurately. Details belong in
conversation and in a handout.

## Content (pp. 224, 227)

- Keep the IMRaD order, with your own judgment about what each section needs.
- **Introduction** becomes a short statement of the basic questions and hypotheses. If the study
  follows up earlier work, cite that work, briefly; full APA-style citation of a literature takes
  space the poster does not have.
- **Method** shrinks to the general nature of participants, materials and procedure. A photo of
  the apparatus can replace a paragraph.
- **Results** gain the most from graphics. A figure lets a viewer see the result at a glance.
- **Conclusions** can be short bullet points.
- If the hypotheses need more space, take it from Method. The message comes first.

## Visual style (pp. 225–227)

- Legible from 1–1.2 m (3–4 ft). Typical sizes: title about 48 pt; section headings 36 pt; body
  text 18–24 pt.
- Check the conference’s board size before designing. Allotted space runs from about 1 × 1.3 m
  to 1.3 × 2 m.
- Dark text on a light background is easiest to read for a long time. A dark background with
  light text draws the eye but tires readers.
- Readers move top-left, down the column, then to the top of the next column. Lay out sections
  in that order.
- Multi-sheet posters: group related sheets, use a coloured backing sheet as a border, and keep
  to two or three colours (Figures 16.1–16.2).

## Software (pp. 230–235, Tables 16.1–16.2)

The book walks through building a single-sheet or multi-sheet poster in PowerPoint: page
size, gridlines, a banner text box for the title (up to 72 pt), text boxes for each section,
and charts and tables pasted from Excel or Word. Any slide or layout tool works the same way.

## At the session (pp. 228–230)

- Dress professionally for the venue; comfortable shoes, since sessions last one to two hours
  on hard floors. Viewers judge the work partly by the presenter.
- Expect most people to pass by. The format lets the few with shared interests talk to you.
- Stand to one side of the poster so you do not block it. Stay open to visitors; a long chat
  with a friend tells them not to interrupt.
- If you cannot answer a question, say so and address the issue as far as you can.
- Bring a handout: a small copy of the poster or a short manuscript, in an envelope on the board
  or on a chair.
- Walk the session before it starts, or after the crowd thins, to see other posters.

## Later design advice (reading list)

Faulkes (2021) and Morrison’s (2019) “#BetterPoster” layout put the main finding in one
plain-language sentence in large type at the centre, with a QR code to the full paper. Many
conferences now accept this layout. Check the venue’s guidelines.
