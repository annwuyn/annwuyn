# 4 — Dependency, clauses and downgrading

Source: sessions S12–S18. Dependency is *which idea rides on which*. Getting it wrong produces
sentences that are grammatical and still wrong.

## Ranked types

**semantic > grammatical > positional**
(frequency of use runs the other way: semantic < positional < grammatical)

- **Semantic** — explicit importance marking: *More importantly,…* / *What's more significant
  is that…*. Overrides everything.
- **Grammatical** — main vs subordinate clause; overrides position.
- **Positional** — earlier/later, central/peripheral. Weakest, but free.

## Grammatical roles and what they do

| Role | Weight | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| coordinating conjunction (`conj/co`) | coordinates | *and, nor, neither, either, or, so, for, but, yet, plus* |
| subordinating conjunction (`conj/sub`) | subordinates | links a subordinate clause to a main clause |
| adverb (`adv`) | coordinates | most position-flexible → watch scope and distance |
| preposition (`prep`) | subordinates | + NP/AdvP |

Same relation, three weights:
- *Since his wife was sick, he cooked dinner.* (sub → main)
- *His wife was sick so that he cooked dinner.* (main ← sub)
- *His wife was sick, and so | and therefore he cooked dinner.* (↔ independent)

`so that` [subconj] vs `so` [coconj] vs `therefore` [adv]:
- *People are living longer so that they should be allowed to work longer.* (main ← sub)
- *People are living longer, so they should be allowed to work longer.* (↔ independent)
- *People are living longer. Therefore, they should be allowed…* (↔ sentences)
- *People are living longer; therefore | so, they should…* / *…, [and] therefore they should…*
  — with a comma, `and` is optional but advisable for coordination and role clarity.
- *People are living longer and therefore should be allowed…* (↔ compound verbs, ellipsis)

`because, given that` [subconj] vs `for` [L, coconj]:
*I couldn't stay because the area was violent.* (main ← sub) vs
*I couldn't stay[,] for the area was violent.* (↔ independent) — grammatical importance
overrides positional.

## because / since / as — the revised rule

Standard advice (Swan) says `since`/`as` when the reason is already known to both parties,
`because` when only the speaker knows. The course reframes it by **linearity**:

- reason **already known** to the reader (old information) → start the sentence with it
  (marked; `since`/`as` preferred);
- reason **unknown** (new information) → start with the **main clause** (unmarked; `because`).

Why: `A [S1]. B because A'(=A) [S2]` is a lexical Rheme-Rheme.
`A [S1]. Because A'(=A), B [S2]` is a lexical Rheme-Theme. The second flows.

`because` as a textual Theme (sentence-initial, standing alone) is acceptable only in speech.
Register ladder for the same relation:
`because / since / as` [N] → `given that` [A] → `on the grounds that` [LW].
Always unmarked: *especially as* / *particularly since*.

`because` [conj] vs `because of` [prep] leaves dependency unchanged.
`due to` is adjectival, `because of`/`owing to` adverbial — `due to` is far more frequent after
*to be*. (Modern usage increasingly treats them interchangeably.)

## Comma rules for dependency

- **Subordinate, marked** (sub before main) → use a comma.
  *Since the gorilla is very powerful, it has no real enemies.*
- **Subordinate, unmarked** (sub after main) → generally no punctuation.
  *The gorilla has no real enemies since it is very powerful.*
- **Independent clauses** → comma always correct; optional if short and well balanced.

The comma is *the yellow light*, and it is also **a means of defining linking scope**.

## Clause types

```
Independent (main)
Dependent (subordinate)
├── Relative (adjectival)
├── Non-relative
│   ├── Nominal
│   └── Adverbial
└── Absolute
```
Features that cross-cut: restrictive/non-restrictive · reduced · embedded · finite/non-finite.

**Relative.** Restrictive prefers `that` (`that` > `which`, BR > AM); non-restrictive takes
`which`. *John has a daughter who is a teacher* [defining] vs *John has a daughter, who is a
teacher* [non-defining]. Formality ladder: *with whom I spent hours talking* > *whom I spent
hours talking with* > *who I…* > *[that] I…*.

**Reduction.**
- restrictive, normal verb: *Schools that allow…* → *Schools **allowing**…* (subject reduced);
  *The watch that you gave me* → *The watch you gave me* (object reduced)
- restrictive, auxiliary *be*: *Schools that are located…* → *Schools **located**…*
- non-restrictive, same-subject rule: *My sister, who works as a receptionist, usually takes the
  bus* → *My sister, **working** as a receptionist, usually takes the bus*
- adverbial: *when I travel by train* → *when **travelling** by train* (same-subject rule;
  exceptions *when possible*, *when necessary*)
- nominal: `that`-clause → possessive/objective + gerund
  (*She mentions **your becoming** interested…* > *…**you becoming**…*)

**Absolute** — no explicit linking item, always reduced, [F, O, L]:
*The weather being bad, I can't go out.* · *His voice trembling, he told his story.* ·
*He stood before me, his hands in his pockets.*

**Embedded** — a subordinate clause placed mid-sentence so it is **thematically non-intrusive**.
This is the workhorse of downgrading.

**Non-finite** — identify the implied subject, then treat as finite:
*She arrived early in order to [she] get a good seat.*

**Comma splice** — three fixes: comma + conjunction · semicolon · absolute clause.

## Downgrading

Demote material that should not compete for the Theme position, without losing it.

| From | To | Example |
|---|---|---|
| clause | simple modifier | *It is reported that more than 70% … suffered* → *More than 70% … have **reportedly** suffered* |
| independent clause | subordinate clause | *Clearly obtaining financial knowledge at college was my first step… Then my internship at E&Y…* → ***While** a finance degree was…, my E&Y internship…* |
| 2-verb VN sequence | 1 verb + adverb | *lựa chọn và đầu tư* → ***selectively invest*** · *cười và chấp nhận* → ***smilingly accepted*** · *gạt nước mắt mà đi* → ***tearfully left*** · *biết mà vẫn vi phạm* → ***knowingly violated*** |
| sentence | non-restrictive embedded clause | *Tom is smiling and waving at Kate. I believe that he doesn't like her. This sudden change…* → *Tom, **who, I believe, doesn't like Kate**, is smiling and waving at her. This sudden change…* |
| separate clause | reduced participial | *Thong Nhat Park is located at 354 Le Duan Street. This park attracts…* → *Thong Nhat Park, **located at 354 Le Duan Street**, attracts…* |
| redundant adjective | non-restrictive clause | *The other **invalid** reason is Y* → *The other given reason, **which is invalid**, is Y* |

Dashes also downgrade: *This is from — it came as a surprise to me — a Stanford professor.*

Downgrading interacts with TP: an embedded non-restrictive clause carries information
**without interfering with the main TP progression**. That is the whole point.

## Factoring out and ellipsis

- **Ellipsis**: `(A→C) + (B→C)` ► `(A→C) + (B→Ø)`
- **Factoring out**: `(A→C) + (B→C)` ► `(A+B)→C` or `C→(A+B)`
- Deeper: `(A→C→D) + (B→C→E)` ► `C→[(A→D) + (B→E)]`

> *Both she lives in New Orleans and her sister lives in New Orleans* ►
> *Both she and her sister live in New Orleans.*
> *Either the results of the research are impressive or the results are alarming* ►
> *The results of the research are either impressive or alarming.*

**Correlative pairs** (COMBINATION, parallelism required):
`both…and…` · `either…or…` · `neither…nor…` · `not only…but also…` · `whether…or…`
Note: `not only` and `but also` are **not** grammatically parallel to each other.
Inversion for emphasis: *Not only was it raining … but the band was also late.*

`either/or` can be COMBINATION or ALTERNATIVE depending on discourse:
*"We can have either beef or chicken."* (combination — both on the table) vs
*"Or we may try beef."* (alternative).

## English mood

`indicative` [fact] · `interrogative` [question] · `imperative` [command] ·
`conditional` [condition] · `subjunctive` [wish, hypothetical] — the speaker/writer's intention.

Full subjunctive sets the mental state in a hypothetical world (*If I **were** a doctor…*);
indicative alternatives set it in the real one (*If I **am** a doctor…*). The three overlap:
*If I was a doctor, I would try…* [conditional type 2] vs *If I were…* [subjunctive present]
vs *If I am…* [indicative present]. Partial subjunctive keeps the subordinate verb tenseless:
*The girl demanded | demands | will demand that her boyfriend **buy** her a diamond ring.*

`in case` [N, indicative, possibility] vs `lest` [F, O, L, subjunctive, worrisomeness] vs
`for fear that` [conj, indicative] / `for fear of` [prep].
