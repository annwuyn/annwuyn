# 6 — Appraisal and Engagement

Source: sessions S34–S35, *Engagement resources* handout. A simplified adaptation of Martin's
Appraisal model for practical use.

An effective argument does not simply voice opinions. It **engages readers**, inviting them to
support the viewpoint, and **anticipates objections**, presenting the text as an ongoing
conversation.

## Appraisal has three components

- **Attitude** — feelings: emotional reactions (*I'm sad*), judgement of behaviour (*You make
  people sad*), evaluation of things (*This movie is sad*).
- **Engagement** — the writer's degree of commitment (**writer intentionality**) and
  anticipation of readers' responses (**audience awareness**). ← the argumentative-genre lever
- **Graduation** — degree of evaluation (*a **very** vs **truly** vs **somewhat** sad movie*).

## FACT vs PROPOSITION

- *The US has 50 states.* — fact, non-arguable, no Engagement resources
- *The US **is reported to** have 50 states.* — proposition, Attribute
- *The US, **I believe**, has 50 states.* — proposition, Entertain

## The taxonomy

```
Monogloss  ── Narrative
           └─ (bare assertion)

Heterogloss
├── EXPAND (open up the argument space)
│   ├── Entertain — Modal (modal verb · conditional · possibility · frequency · scope)
│   │             · Opinion · Postulation · Rhetorical Question
│   └── Attribute — Specific Source · Common Wisdom · Hearsay
└── CONTRACT (restrict the argument space)
    ├── Disclaim — Deny (negation) · Counter (contrastive & counter-expectational)
    └── Proclaim — Concur (Affirmative · Leading Question · Concede)
                 · Endorse (factivity verbs; Specific Source / Common Wisdom / Hearsay)
                 · Pronounce
```

### Realisations

| Category | Typical wording |
|---|---|
| **Entertain: Modal Verb** | could, may, will, should, must; had better, ought to, need to, be able to, have to |
| **Entertain: Conditional** | if, unless, once, suppose; other things being equal |
| **Entertain: Possibility** | perhaps, maybe, definitely; it's likely/possible/almost certain that; chances are, little chance, no hope; tend to |
| **Entertain: Frequency** | often, usually, sometimes, always; in some cases |
| **Entertain: Scope** | partly, mostly, virtually, in general; in some way, to some extent |
| **Entertain: Opinion** | in my view/opinion; I think/believe/suspect/submit/am convinced that |
| **Entertain: Postulation** | apparently, presumably; it seems/appears/looks like; the evidence/research suggests that |
| **Entertain: Rhetorical Q** | *Is that true?* |
| **Attribute** | X said/stated/believed/claimed that; X's assertion/belief that; according to X, in X's view |
| **— Specific Source** | *President Obama states that…*, *according to the New York Times* |
| **— Common Wisdom** | *as a proverb has it…*, *common wisdom dictates that…*, *it is generally assumed that…* |
| **— Hearsay** | *some people / most businessmen*, *according to statistics*, *reportedly*, *there's an argument that*, *it is rumoured that* |
| **Disclaim: Deny** | no, not, nothing, never; don't |
| **Disclaim: Counter** | although, however, but, yet, while; that said, in fact, the very; even, only, just, still, already, actually, surprisingly, strangely enough; how else; as if, anyway; turn out; do/does/did |
| **Concur: Affirmative** | of course, obviously, no wonder; naturally, unsurprisingly; everyone knows that |
| **Concur: Leading Q** | *Who doesn't want a million dollars?* |
| **Concur: Concede** | admittedly, arguably, certainly; understandably, justifiably; granted, yes; at least, just, at first, except for |
| **Endorse** | demonstrate, prove, show, point out; rule, conclude, know, agree (factivity verbs) |
| **Pronounce** | I'd say / I contend / I insist that; it is clear/obvious to me that; you must agree; the fact/truth of the matter is; there can be no doubt that; intensifiers with clausal scope — really, indeed, in fact, simply |

Boundary cases worth memorising:
- Modals of permission/obligation (*You must turn off the light*) are **Entertain**; the bare
  imperative (*Turn off the light*) is **monoglossic**.
- *certainly* = Entertain (*She is certainly beautiful*) **or** Concede (*Certainly he is rude
  but look at his qualifications!*).
- *in fact* = Pronounce **or** Counter (*I want to say it was good, but, in fact, it was not*).
- *Clearly* = Concur; *It is clear to me that* = Pronounce.
- **Endorse vs Attribute** — both draw on external sources, but Endorse is dialogically
  **contractive**, Attribute **expansive**:
  *Many studies **demonstrate / prove**…* (Endorse) vs *…**suggest / claim / report**…* (Attribute).
- **Narrative** is a special form of monogloss: no overt argument, so it makes no allowance for
  counter-argument — but it *gently guides readers toward the supported viewpoint while
  preempting objections unobtrusively*. Powerful in scholarship essays.

## The five calibration targets

For Vietnamese writers moving toward native academic norms:

1. **HETEROGLOSS : MONOGLOSS ↑**
2. **POSTULATION : OPINION ↑**
3. **SPECIFIC SOURCE : COMMON WISDOM / HEARSAY ↑**
4. **PRONOUNCE ↓**
5. **NARRATIVES & RHETORICAL QUESTIONS ↑**

### Why each

1. Vietnamese and ESL essays use monogloss far more than American essays → writers impose their
   own viewpoint and assume reader agreement. Bare assertions especially: they assume greater
   writer–reader "like-mindedness" and rely more on implicit links. They also risk unfounded
   statements (*Firstly, children's brains are blank when they were born.*).
2. OPINION (subjective) vs POSTULATION (objective observation) are opposite support manners.
   Vietnamese and ESL writers overuse Opinion and underuse Postulation, making their own voice
   rhetorically prominent instead of the evidence. American writers play down the writer's role.
3. American expert writers clearly prefer SPECIFIC SOURCES; Vietnamese expert writers rely
   heavily on COMMON WISDOM (proverbs, *người xưa đã nhắc nhở…*), drawing attention away from
   the individual toward broader community views.
   When you have no citable source, use **pseudo-specific sources**: *The present study draws on
   the work of…* / *I shall make reference to…* / *A number of sources are cited, including,
   among others,…*
4. Pronounce foregrounds the writer as an authority; English academic prose downplays the
   writer's role in interpreting knowledge.
5. Well-placed rhetorical questions make the text interactive and strengthen writer–reader
   solidarity.

## Pre-counterargument concession

Temporarily give ground before taking it back. The single highest-value move.

> *Brinkley mentions that Vietnam fought a lot of wars. However, most of them are not waged by us.*
> → *…**While this is technically true**, most of them are not waged by us.*

> *Building a new parking lot would be costly. But there is an urgent need for the facility.*
> → ***Granted | Admittedly**, building a new parking lot may be costly. But there is an urgent
> need…* (also *Be that as it may* [N→F], *Fair enough, but* [I→N])

> *Brinkley appears subjective in his argument, aiming to impose his own thoughts onto readers.*
> → *…and, **whether intentionally or not, seems to give the impression of** imposing…*

Softening over-strong Pronounce:
- *While I agree with you that…* / *While some of your complaints seem valid…*
  (not *While I cannot deny all your complaints…*)

## Verb choice carries Engagement

- *I do not agree with several **reasons** cited by Brinkley for **concluding** that…*
  → *…for **claiming** that…* (don't Endorse your opponent's inference)
- *…as you **pointed out** in your article* → *…as **assumed** in your article*
- *Joel Brinkley **proved** "Vietnamese aggressive tendencies"* → *…**made a speculation about**
  the so-called Vietnamese aggressive tendencies*
- *a statement that **would be considered** illogical* → *a statement that **seems illogical to
  me***
- **assumption** [N, made without proof → ATTRIBUTE] vs **supposition** [F, testable and
  provable → ENDORSE]

## Analysis output format

When analysing a text for Engagement, produce four passes:

1. **Non-arguable facts** — list them
2. **Monoglossic vs heteroglossic** passages — mark and comment on the style each implies
3. **Categorise monoglossic** propositions — note the background knowledge assumed but unstated
4. **Categorise heteroglossic** propositions with inline tags and an Expand/Contract split:

> *Zhang Ning **is supposed** [Attribute:Hearsay] to be the best female badminton player of all
> time, … **in my opinion** [Entertain:Opinion] … **I know** [Proclaim:Concur] … **but**
> [Disclaim:Counter] … she **never** [Disclaim:Deny] outdid Susi Susanti … I **still**
> [Disclaim:Counter] sometimes watch … **Even** [Disclaim:Counter] now … **not least**
> [Disclaim:Deny] in their illustration of…*

Then write the interpretation: what stance does this distribution construct, and where does the
writer stand relative to the reader?
