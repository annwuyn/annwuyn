# 1 — Linearity

Source: sessions S1–S5. The foundation. Everything else is a technique for
*serving* linearity; nothing else can rescue a text that lacks it.

Linearity is a property of the **idea chain**, not of sentences. It is independent of
sentence boundaries — a single sentence can be non-linear, and a five-sentence stretch
can be perfectly linear.

## The seven criteria

A linear text is:

| Criterion | Fails when |
|---|---|
| **logically ordered** | ideas appear in an order the reasoning does not license |
| **gap-free** | a step the reader needs is missing (unstated bridging idea) |
| **repetition-free** | the same idea reappears in different wording |
| **digression-free** | material that belongs to no thread is present |
| **bi-directional** | you cannot read the chain backwards and still recover the logic |
| **open to multiple interpretations** | the chain forces one reading where the writer meant several — or admits readings the writer did not mean |
| **component-integral** | a link joins wholes that are not comparable wholes |

Marked `[*]` in the course as the three most frequently violated by Vietnamese writers:
gap-free, repetition-free, bi-directional.

## The re-arrangement procedure

Run this on any paragraph. Do **not** rewrite prose until the chain is settled.

1. **Sieve the ideas.** Break the text into numbered atomic idea units `(1) (2) (3)…`.
   Ignore sentence boundaries; a relative clause is its own unit.
2. **Disregard cohesive devices.** Strip every linking word first. They are the writer's
   claim about the logic, not the logic. `Besides`, `Therefore`, `Moreover` routinely
   paper over a chain that does not connect.
3. **Consolidate repetitions.** Merge units that say the same thing in different words:
   `(2)+(6)`, `(4)+(7)`. *Same idea, different wording* is the single most common defect.
4. **Eliminate digressions.**
5. **Unpack implicit links.** Insert the missing bridging ideas as `(*)`. Vietnamese
   writers rely far more on implicit links than English readers tolerate.
6. **Re-order.** Produce the chain: `(*) → (4) → (2) → (3) → (1)`.
7. Only now write the prose.

**Linking principle.** If two apparently independent ideas are to be addressed in the same
thread, their linking must be made explicit.

## Model complexity

- **Simplified** — one straight chain.
- **Branching** — `A → {B, C}`; check whether the branches are *independent items* or
  *connected items*. Connected items must be sequenced, not listed.
- **Sub-grouping** — `A(a1→…→an) → B(b1→…→bk) → C(…)`. Each subgroup is internally
  linear; the subgroups are linear among themselves. This is what an essay *is*:
  Intro → Body(P1…Pq) → Conclusion, each paragraph a subgroup.
- **Dependency interaction** — a *local violation* of overall linearity, traded for better
  dependency, flow, or sentence complexity. Legitimate, but only after the base chain is
  correct. The course's explicit caveat: **do not take shortcuts yet.**

## Ordering patterns inside a subgroup

There is no single correct pattern; focus and transparency are the goals, and each
subgroup may take a different one. For an argumentative paragraph:

- overall → argument structure (claim / ground / warrant / rebuttal)
- rationale → logical sequence
- between examples → ranking
- inside one example → time sequence

Lists are not exempt. Whenever there is a list, re-order it. Test whether items are
independent or connected: *playing games with grandparents · visiting grandparents ·
buying video games · asking grandparents for money* has a right order and several wrong ones.

## Diagnostic patterns (memorise these two)

**Mistake 1** — `A → (leads to) B → (also caused by) C`
Fix: `(A + C) → (both lead to) B`. Two causes were strung out as a chain.

**Mistake 2** — `A → (caused by) B → (A then causes) C`
Fix: `B → A → C`. The chain was entered in the middle.

Both are extremely common under test conditions.

## Cause weight vs cause timeline

When several causes feed one effect, rank them twice — by **weight** (importance) and by
**timeline** — then let the ranking choose the dependency structure. Different rankings
produce different, all-grammatical, sentences that foreground different causes.

> *Despite being a reputable Stanford professor (1) and winning the Pulitzer (2), Joel
> Brinkley disappoints many readers (3) because he delivered a discriminating argument (4).*
> weight: (2) < (1) < (4) · timeline: (1) > (2) > (4)
> → *Joel Brinkley disappoints many readers (3) because he, a reputable Stanford professor
> of journalism (1), who won the Pulitzer Prize in 1980 (2), delivered a discriminating
> argument (4).*

## Why start here

Linearity re-arrangement fixes linking, dependency, grammar, paraphrasing, redundancy and
sentence complexity **at once**. Treating those as separate problems is why editing
Vietnamese-English drafts sentence-by-sentence does not work.

Also: linear but not necessarily thematically progressive. Linearity is necessary,
not sufficient — see `02-thematic-progression.md`.
