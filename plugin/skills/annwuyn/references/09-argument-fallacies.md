# 9 — Argument structure, rebuttal and fallacies

Source: sessions S35–S37.

## Toulmin model

| Component | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | the viewpoint being argued for |
| **Ground / data** | reasons and specific evidence supporting the claim |
| **Warrant** | the underlying assumption connecting grounds to claim |
| **Backing** | justification for the warrant |
| **Rebuttal** | description and rebuttal of counter-arguments |
| **Qualifier** | specification of limits to claim, warrant and backing |

Worked example — *Harry doesn't have FOUR brothers*:

- **Claim** Harry doesn't have four brothers
- **Ground** Harry has three brothers in the family picture
- **Warrant** W1 a family picture *should* have all members · W2 if it is three, it can't be four
- **Backing** B1 dictionary definition of *family picture* · B2 quantification is an attribute
  that can have only one value at a time
- **Counter/Rebuttal** one brother could be absent → *the note on the back says "the whole
  family"*; one brother had not been born yet
- **Qualifier** at the time the picture was taken

**QUALIFIER** narrows the scope of the argument; **REBUTTAL** supplies additional
evidence/rationale. Don't conflate them.

Rebut when evidence can refute an objection (a caption shows the whole family was there); qualify
when it cannot (a brother born after the photo). In Toulmin’s own layout the qualifier marks the
force of the step from data to claim (*presumably*) and the rebuttal states the conditions of
exception; the course’s rebuttal (counterargument plus answer) follows composition textbooks.
Say which sense you mean. More worked analyses: `14-lecture-notes.md` §14.

## Warrants

- normally **implicit**
- probably a complete argument in its own right
- associated with **greater risks of fallacy**
- the key to Critical/Verbal Reasoning and Argument essays
- **a particular problem for Vietnamese writers**, whose warrants often stay unstated

A warrant is often a **PRINCIPLE**: *After suffering food poisoning → clients are unlikely to
visit the restaurant again* rests on *Once burned, twice shy*. Watch for **rival principles**
(*Never, never give up*) — that is where Demonstration of Irrelevance bites.

## Rebuttal

Purpose: demonstrate that the writer (a) is aware of opposing views and is not ignoring them,
(b) is thinking about readers' responses and anticipating their objections, and (c) has a
viewpoint made **stronger** by being contrasted with the counter-arguments.

Three approaches:

1. **Strategic concession** — acknowledge some merits of a different view in order to *qualify*
   the supported viewpoint.
2. **Refutation** — show important shortcomings of the opponent's argument and reject directly.
3. **Demonstration of irrelevance** — the opposing view may be valid in certain respects but
   does not apply to this situation.

## Issue essays

**ARGUMENTATIVE** = argue about how the world *is* (*logging is an environmental problem*).
**PERSUASIVE** = argue about how the world *should be* (*persuade the reader to act*).
**ONE-SIDED** = present one side. **TWO-SIDED** = evaluate two or more opposing positions and
judge which is more viable.

General structure:

```
INTRODUCTION   thesis statement + take stand · recommended action (persuasive)
BODY           arguments + evidence (1st perspective)
               arguments + evidence (2nd perspective)          [two-sided]
               counter-arguments + evidence + rebuttals        [one-sided, optional]
               evaluation and position                          [two-sided]
CONCLUSION     reinforcement of thesis · recommended action (persuasive) · NO new information
```

| Prompt type | Genre | Body |
|---|---|---|
| **Agreeing or disagreeing** | argumentative, one-sided | arguments + evidence; counter-arguments + rebuttals (optional) |
| **Giving an explanation** | argumentative, one-sided | arguments + evidence |
| **Offering a solution** | persuasive, one-sided | intro adds recommended action |
| **Stating a preference** | argumentative, two-sided | both perspectives, then evaluation and position |

## Idea map and the 30-minute plan

```
Thesis statement
 └ Point 1 (topic sentence) → Rationale → Specific evidence     [linear development]
 └ Point 2 (topic sentence) → Rationale → Specific evidence     [linear development]
```

- **5 min** outlining: take a stand · idea map · 2–3 topic sentences
- **20 min** writing: introduction & conclusion, body
- **5 min** revising

## Manner of exemplification

> *It is important for you to AVOID overly general examples, or lists of examples without
> expansion.* — GRE Official Guide

> *A writer can earn a high score by giving multiple examples or by presenting a single,
> extended example… What matters is NOT the number of examples, the number of paragraphs, or
> the form your argument takes but, rather, the cogency of your ideas and the clarity and skill
> with which you communicate those ideas to academic readers.* — GRE Official Guide

Distinguish **rationale** from **specific example**. This is a rationale, not an example:
> *For example, if parents lead a healthy life in harmony, the youngsters will definitely learn
> good things. By contrast, if parents smoke, drink, and behave badly, they will absolutely
> affect their children negatively.*

The course's Text A/Text B comparison: 2 elaborated examples beat 7 unexpanded ones.

## Template problems

Templates produce contradictory signposting:
> *Admittedly … A (positive)… **In contrast** B (negative)… **On the other hand**, B (more
> positive) vs A…*
> → *On the one hand, A's advantages over B. On the other hand, B's advantages over A.*

Inter-clausal/sentential cohesive devices are **signposts for text flow**. If they point the
wrong way, the reader follows them and gets lost.

## Common fallacies

| Fallacy | Core error | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Correlation** | confuse correlation with causation | *An Olympic gold medalist eats this cereal. Therefore if I eat it I can become a champion.* · *Gun ownership went up in Florida and crime went down; so encourage gun ownership.* |
| **Post hoc** | confuse sequence with causation | *Bill installed new software; his computer then froze; the software caused it.* |
| **Common cause** | confuse a common cause with causation | *Bill wakes with a fever, then finds red spots; the fever caused the spots.* |
| **Sufficiency vs Necessity** | invert a conditional | *If my car has gas, it can run. Hence if it can't run, it must have no gas.* |
| **Sampling error** | biased samples | *Polls in FL, CA, TX → 55 % of all Americans.* · *Jane was cheated by three ex-boyfriends; men lie.* |
| **Begging the question** | assume the truth of the argument in proving it | *The belief in God is universal, because everyone believes in God.* |
| **Circular argument** | | *God exists → the Bible says so → the Bible was written by God.* |
| **Slippery slope** | suppositions about the future as evidence | *One donut → habit → weight gain → diabetes.* |
| **Appeal to authority** | reputation of an irrelevant third party as main evidence | *Pacifism must be right since Einstein supported it.* |
| **Appeal to common belief** | truth by consensus | *80 % of Americans believe in God, so God exists.* |
| **Appeal to consequences of a belief** | assume truth to avoid a bad conclusion | *If God did not exist, all morality would be lost.* |
| **Straw man** | address a misrepresented version | *He says no budget increase for education → he doesn't care about future generations.* |
| **Red herring** | draw attention from the main issue | *It's not about the death penalty; it's about victims and their rights.* |
| **Personal attack** | attack the person, not the claim | *That story can't be true — Tom supports it.* |
| **False dilemma** | fail to consider all possibilities | *You are not her mother, how can she be your daughter?* |
| **Burden of proof** | argue from lack of counter-evidence | *You cannot prove God does not exist, so He does.* |
| **Composition / Division** | assume correspondence between a set and its members | *Every player is a superstar, so the team is great.* · *Tom lives in a large building, so his apartment is large.* · *X's revenue grew $10 m vs Y's $1 m, so X is more successful.* |

## Fallacy-handling procedure

1. **Identify the warrant.**
2. **Explain why it is wrong.**
3. **Supply specific evidence** to either invalidate or improve the argument.

That third step is what separates a fallacy-spotting exercise from an argument. Always ask:
*what kind of evidence would be needed to improve or invalidate this?*

## Evaluating a given argument

Split the argument into sub-arguments and take the two you can attack most fully. For each: the
data, the claim drawn, the warrant, why it fails, and the evidence that would repair or collapse
it. Explain the faulty reasoning in plain words; labels such as *composition* vary between
sources and prove nothing on their own. In critical-reasoning items, name the main fallacy and
the task (strengthen or weaken) before reading the options.

## Test focus

- **TOEFL/IELTS** → focus more on **HOW**: writing mechanics, format, knowledge of writing
  conventions; more fixed in form. Idea (one main idea + rationales + specific examples),
  organization (linearity, linking items, scope, dependency), vocabulary range and idiom,
  sentence complexity.
- **GRE/LSAT** → focus more on **WHAT**: manner of argument, relevant evidence, audience
  awareness; levels of critical thinking; more flexible in form.
  Claim–ground–warrant–rebuttal–qualifier; engagement resources; fallacies; relevancy and
  manner of exemplification.
- Both require academic vocabulary, a fairly formal style, and **linear development**.

Formats changed after the course was recorded (checked November 2026; details and sources in
`13-research-update.md` §10). GRE writing is one *Analyze an Issue* task (30 minutes) since
September 2023; the *Analyze an Argument* essay is gone, but its skills still serve GRE and GMAT
critical-reasoning questions and every research argument. LSAT Argumentative Writing (since July

prewriting. The GMAT Focus Edition has no essay. TOEFL iBT Writing (since January 2026) is
*Build a Sentence*, *Write an Email* and *Write for an Academic Discussion*, scored 1 to 6.
