# 3 — Cohesion and conjunctive relations

Source: sessions S9–S12. See `linking-items.md` for the 219-entry lookup table.

**Coherence** is the underlying semantic connection. **Cohesion** is its surface marking.
Different languages realise the same coherence through different cohesion — that is Kaplan's
point. Coherent ≠ cohesive: a text can be perfectly coherent to its writer and uncohesive
to its reader.

## The five cohesive device types

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **reference** | pronouns, demonstratives pointing to a referent | *The dog ate the bird and **it** died.* |
| **substitution** | replacing an element, but not with a personal pronoun | *"Do you have a computer?" "I used to own **one**."* · *"I think **so**."* |
| **ellipsis** | deleting a repeated element | *He wrote a letter and [he] sent it home.* |
| **conjunction** | logical relations between parts of a text | ← the course's focus |
| **lexical cohesion** | subclassification, synonymy/antonymy, part/whole | *Cats … more frequently than dogs. **Animals** are usually affected as…* |

Reference splits into **endophoric** (referent inside the text: *anaphoric* backwards,
*cataphoric* forwards) and **exophoric** (referent outside the text — *the Queen*, *VTV3*).
Exophoric reference is culturally loaded and a frequent source of cross-cultural failure.

Lexical cohesion is heavily culture-influenced, so **TP analysis must stay culturally
independent** — which means lexical cohesion tends to produce more New-Theme readings.
Linearity helps decode it; lexical cohesion + reference together help maintain TP continuity.

## The 10 basic conjunctive relationships

Weight matters as much as meaning. `A = B` equal weight; `A > B` A more important.

| # | Relation | Meaning | Weight | Sample items |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Combination** | A and B considered together | A = B | and, furthermore, similarly |
| 2 | **Alternative** | A and B are alternatives | A = B | alternatively, or |
| 3 | **Sequence** | time or logical sequence | A = B | when, before, in the first place, then |
| 4 | **Paraphrase** | B restates A differently | A = B | in other words, that is to say |
| 5 | **Amplification** | B describes A in more detail | A > B | to be more specific |
| 6 | **Exemplification** | B is an example of A | A > B | for example, such as |
| 7 | **Summary** | B summarises A | B > A | to sum up, in short, in a word |
| 8 | **Cause-Effect** | A is cause or effect of B | B > A | because, since, thus, as a result, so that |
| 9 | **Contrast** | B is contrary to the expectation raised by A | B > A | but, although, yet, however, still, nevertheless |
| 10 | **Exclusion** | B excludes A | B > A | instead, on the contrary, rather than |

Containment: PURPOSE ∈ CAUSE/EFFECT · EXCEPTION ∈ EXCLUSION · EXEMPLIFICATION ∈ AMPLIFICATION.

**No one-to-one mapping between item and relation.** *furthermore* = combination *or*
amplification. *then* = sequence *or* amplification *or* cause-effect. Always resolve the
relation from the text, then choose the item — never the reverse.

SEQUENCE splits into **time** and **logic**:
> *First, she cried in her sister's arms. Next, she sat alone…* (time)
> *First, it meant liberation from marriage. Next, it gave her financial independence.* (logic)

Coordinating relations (equal weight): combination, alternative.
Subordinating: **amplification** puts the most important component *first*
(*Tom's rich. In addition, he's handsome.*); **contrast** makes the *second* component more
important (*You're a good student but you've made a lot of mistakes* ≠ *You've made a lot of
mistakes but you're a good student*).

Compare: *capable yet old* vs *old yet capable*. *poor yet proud* vs *proud yet poor*.

## Problematic pairs to police

- COMBINATION vs AMPLIFICATION (coordination vs subordination)
- CONTRAST vs EXCLUSION
- PARAPHRASE vs CAUSE/EFFECT (necessity & sufficiency)
- SEQUENCE vs CAUSE/EFFECT

## Conjunction–adverb combination

`[conj] + [adv]` can carry two relations at once:

| Combination | Relations |
|---|---|
| *and, specifically* | COMBINATION + AMPLIFICATION |
| *yet at the same time* | CONTRAST + SEQUENCE |
| *especially since* | AMPLIFICATION + CAUSE/EFFECT |
| *now that* | SEQUENCE + CAUSE/EFFECT |
| *even though* | AMPLIFICATION + CONTRAST |
| *So in other words* | CAUSE/EFFECT + PARAPHRASE |

## Documented misuse checklist

Every item below is a real error pattern from the course. Check a draft against it.

1. **`on the contrary` vs `in contrast`** — routinely swapped. `on the contrary` = EXCLUSION
   (rejecting what was just said); `in contrast` = CONTRAST (setting two things side by side,
   with parallelism and lexical cohesion implied). In dialogue the choice depends on the
   implicit link: *"The meeting was terrible." "On the contrary, I enjoyed it."* (B rejects A's
   assessment) vs *In contrast* (B is contrasting two different experiences).
2. **`in contrast` where EXCLUSION is meant** → use `rather` or `instead`.
   *…should not get a job. ~~In contrast~~ **Rather**, they should focus on studying.*
   Note `rather (than)` / `instead` ≠ `alternatively`.
3. **`alternatively` where COMBINATION is meant** → `likewise`. And note the strength ladder:
   `similarly`/`likewise` ≪ `furthermore`/`moreover`.
4. **`on the other hand` on non-parallel ideas.** It demands two genuinely parallel members.
   *In order to protect himself, the black man should be bold… On the other hand, the black
   woman suffered a lot.* — not parallel. Rebuild both halves or drop the frame.
5. **`otherwise` where PARAPHRASE is meant** → `in other words`.
6. **Same-set violations.** `apart from`, `besides`, `other than`, `let alone`, `except`,
   `instead of` all require their two members to belong to one set, correctly ordered.
   - *…enjoyed **other** better services in Asian countries* → *in **other** Asian countries*
     (word-level linearity).
   - *I have not enjoyed any game **let alone** Dota* — inverted; Dota ⊂ games, so the stronger
     term must come second: *I have not enjoyed any games, **Dota in particular*** or
     *I have not enjoyed Dota **or any games for that matter***.
   - *we, **apart from** our diet, could be extremely friendly* → *…**despite** our diet…*
     (diet is not a member of the same set as friendliness).
   - *The overall cost … is appraised at US$200 billion. **Apart from it**, many people died…*
     → the two members are not comparable; recast as
     *…estimated at around US$200 billion. **Apart from these immediate consequences**, the
     catastrophe has also claimed many lives in its long-term aftermath.*
7. **Lexical-category mismatch.** *Dogs deserve to be our best friends **instead of** food.*
   → *Dogs deserve to be **treated as** our best friends, and hence, **not killed for** food.*
8. **Missing bridge before the connective.** A `however` with nothing to hinge on:
   *ALM sees language as a collection of discrete items … However, after years in vogue, ALM is
   challenged…* → insert the bridge: *…**Since this approach has been proven ineffective by
   research**, after years in vogue, ALM, however, is challenged by a new one…*
9. **Long-distance linking.** `A → …distance… → A'(=A) → B` violates the repetition-free rule.
   Do not re-state A to reach B. Start a new paragraph and **paraphrase**:
   *No matter how important to students marks are, they should not be used as an absolute
   criterion…* → then note the redundancy: `no matter how` already contains `however`, so
   dropping the second `however` unless emphasising: *Marks, no matter how important to
   students, should not be used as…*
10. **Colons and semicolons as textual Themes.** `A: B` for amplification relations
    (*…for one reason: you haven't paid your bill*); `A; B` for non-amplification
    (*The rice crop failed; the cotton did quite well.*). Also for quotations with emphasis and
    to resolve comma ambiguity. **Use sparingly.**
