## 1. Course stance and method

- **Linking decides meaning.** A clause read alone can mean the opposite of the same clause in
  its chain: *I did not lose a leg in Vietnam so that I could serve hot dogs to teenagers* (the
  negation scopes over the purpose clause, so he did lose a leg). A public banner split one
  English sentence into two Vietnamese banners and each half misled. Reading or writing
  sentence by sentence fails for this reason. (S1)
- **English readers attach to the nearest candidate.** In *An armed robber shot the sister of the
  actor who was on the balcony*, most English readers link *who* to *the actor*; the Vietnamese
  class chose *the sister*. Two lessons: keep linked items close (→ `05` distance) and leave one
  reading only. (S1, S3)
- **Over-explaining and under-explaining sit side by side.** A Vietnamese email narrates a birth
  certificate, a horoscope and “the city of red flamboyant flowers” where one sentence would do;
  a translated passage names a song without saying why it matters to a foreign reader, then
  spends lines on a phone call and a taxi ride that blur the point. Rule: in narration keep only
  the details that serve the purpose. Seeing this requires stepping outside one’s own culture
  and reading the text as an outsider. (S1)
- **Why the argumentative genre.** Vietnamese school essays (*văn chứng minh*) hand the writer one
  view to prove (*gần mực thì đen*); English prompts present positions to choose between and
  defend. Learners therefore lack the technique and the argument structure at once, and even
  high scorers who rely on templates cannot say what makes an argument sound. (S1)
- **Examiners read once, top to bottom.** The instructor estimates about two minutes per IELTS
  script. A linear text is understood as it is read and judged competent; a looping link is
  scored as weak expression, whatever the vocabulary. Popular test books ask for “a linear
  argument” and never define it; files 01 and 02 supply the definition. (S1, S5)
- **Coherence and cohesion.** Coherence lives in the writer’s head and is almost always fine;
  cohesion is the set of surface signals a reader from another culture can follow. The course
  works on cohesion. (S1)
- **The working order.** Pour every idea onto paper → build the linear chain → connect it with TP
  → choose cohesive devices → rank ideas through dependency → check scope and distance → add the
  argumentative layer (writer intention, a two-way dialogue) → set the register → compress. Most
  learners draft in Vietnamese and push the text into English, so every structural step must be
  done before translating. Slow at first; faster with practice. (S1, S3)
- **Vocabulary is the shallowest layer.** Many online model essays dress naive ideas, linking and
  dependency in big words. Invest in linearity, linking and dependency first. Graders prefer a
  plain text whose line of thought reads easily; teachers stuck at IELTS writing 6 to 6.5
  usually have a linking problem. (S1, S5)
- **Three grammar systems.** Structural (syntax trees: *always quickly kiss Kate* and *always
  kiss Kate quickly* are grammatical, *\*always kiss quickly Kate* is out, since an adverb moves
  within its level of the tree only); functional (the writer’s purpose: why this element first,
  why this linker here; Theme and Rheme live here); cognitive (semantic networks and force
  dynamics, e.g. TO = a path to a goal or limit, then contact and bond: *married to*, *an
  introduction to physics*; FOR adds a hidden purpose or benefit: *invite Mary for dinner*,
  *important for you*). (S1)
- **Argument is a duet.** Narrative is a solo; argument is a duet with an invisible opponent whose
  objection the writer anticipates, answers, then anticipates again. The same skill serves
  interviews. (S1)
- **Kaplan as taught.** Learners who master grammar and vocabulary still read as foreign because
  their sequence of thought runs against native readers’ expectations. Kaplan’s diagrams depict
  surface expression: all people think point to point, and the L1 rhetorical habit reshapes the
  expression. English is mostly linear and uses both deduction and induction, so a deductive or
  inductive pattern cannot identify a writer’s origin. Kaplan himself called English different,
  and the course teaches its patterns because they are the target. Read with `13` §0. (S2)
- **Academic writing is a wide range.** A talk adapted for print (plain words, spoken traces) and a
  dense paper for specialists are both linear academic prose; the IELTS template is one narrow
  point in that range. Learn the criteria and move freely within it. (S2, S4)
- **Learning curve.** The course runs on conscious effort; writing often gets worse before it gets
  better (the U-curve), and the course ends near the bottom of the U. Understanding may arrive
  months or years later. (S1, S37)
- **Keep writing alive.** Practising through dry exam essays kills motivation. Apply the same
  standards to topics you care about (film reviews, forum debates, a rebuttal to someone who
  disagreed with you) and write only a few timed essays before a test. Build your own chain for
  every text, free of templates. (S37)

## 2. Linearity (→ `01`)

- **Where the chain starts.** Start from the unit without which nothing else can happen: in a
  love story, the two people meeting; in a career essay, the half-hidden passion that the rest
  depends on. (S2, S4)
- **Split finely, then strip the connectives.** One sentence may hold three units and two sentences
  one unit. Splitting too finely costs little, since merging later is easy; fused units stay
  hidden. Remove *by, so, it is because* before chaining, or the writer’s faulty linking steers
  the analysis. (S3)
- **Respect the content.** When re-chaining another person’s paragraph, help them express their
  own chain; arguing with the claim is outside the task. (S3)
- **One direction per chain.** A chain may run cause → effect or effect → cause, and keeps one
  direction throughout, including inside each branch. Switching direction inside a paragraph is
  one of the most common errors in IELTS essays. (S3)
- **Hidden units.** Vietnamese texts leave implications unstated (*the doctor says Mum is at risk of
  osteoporosis, so I drink Anlene* hides two links: shared genes, and the mother’s case as a
  warning). If readers can split over whether a hidden unit holds (did the family have a
  refrigerator?), the writer must state it. Vietnamese schooling prizes inference (“the more
  implicit, the deeper”), which makes hidden units hard for Vietnamese writers to see. (S3, S4)
- **Gap-free is relative.** If readers move from A to B unaided, add nothing; if they must ask why A
  leads to B, supply the bridge C. Write for a reader who knows nothing. (S2, S3, S4)
- **Independent or connected?** Before listing reasons, test whether they interact. *Rich and
  handsome* are independent (either can hold alone); *a famous singer with many fans* is
  connected (the fans make the fame) and stays in one chain. Two reasons that only explain the
  result together (no refrigerator; a busy mother) belong in one chain; split into two branches, they explain nothing.
  (S4)
- **Order lists.** Independent items go by importance to the writer; connected items by their
  logical or time relations. Read the writer’s priorities from textual clues: in *A and also B*,
  A ranks higher. With no cause or time order, choose an ordering criterion first (a children’s
  poem about hens moving between house and yard becomes chainable once location is the
  criterion). (S3, S4)
- **Local violations only.** Swapping adjacent units to serve dependency is acceptable (*My friend
  was raised by his father, who was a good teacher*); swapping distant units scrambles the text.
  (S3)
- **Topic sentence and closing paraphrase are format.** The deliberate repetition helps fast
  readers and sits outside the chain: ignore both when chaining, add them back when writing.
  (S3)
- **Read linearly.** Read word by word, predict where the text must go, and stop at the first
  broken link. Vietnamese readers read to the end, infer, find the sentence “fine” and never see
  the fault. The same predictive reading speeds up reading and gap-fill tasks in tests, whose
  texts are logical. (S4)
- **The direction-word skeleton.** Highlight only the words that show direction (*leads to, also
  due to, since, as a result*); patterns such as *A leads to B, B also caused by C* appear at
  once (→ `01` diagnostic patterns). (S4)
- **Polarity after while and but.** After a positive point, *but* or a *while* clause turns
  negative, and the reverse: *Unfortunately, a longer break, while giving students opportunities
  to join various activities, consumes a lot of time and distracts them from their studies.* The
  comma closes the scope of *while* and returns control to *unfortunately*. (S4)
- **Subgrouping.** For many units: decide the groups and their order first, drop each unit into
  its group, chain inside each group, then chain the groups. Typical groups: setting, causes,
  effects (overall effect last); for an argument paragraph, main claim, evidence, alternative
  explanation, with the stronger evidence first (research data before tourists’ anecdotes when
  the opponent’s fault was relying on personal experience). (S5)
- **Direction and importance interact.** A chain that reads well forwards may feel forced backwards
  if the reversal meets the minor cause first; choose the direction that also honours
  importance. (S5)
- **Linearity beyond the paragraph.** It explains much adjective order (*long black hair*: black
  first, grown long later; *dyed long hair*), shows in how one describes a room (start at the
  door and move round), and applies to planning errands and interview answers: list the points
  silently and order them before speaking. (S3, S5, S38)
- **When the text is short.** One-sentence items are the hardest to re-chain because they leave
  the fewest clues. In exams, the more complex the chain, the easier it is to lose linearity
  under time pressure, so split into more paragraphs. (S4, S5)

## 3. Theme, Rheme and thematic progression (→ `02`)

- **Front only with a stated purpose.** Write in natural SVO order unless you can say why you
  fronted an element. A defence lawyer says *Slowly, she turned towards him, gun in hand*
  (hesitation); a prosecutor fronts *Gun in hand*; a neutral reporter keeps the unmarked order.
  Reading the Themes of a text often reveals the speaker’s intention. (S5, S6)
- **Three Theme types, one test.** The topical Theme is compulsory and single; textual and
  interpersonal Themes are optional, must precede it, and anything after the topical Theme is
  Rheme (*It will, as a result, need reviewing*: *as a result* sits in the Rheme). Test a
  candidate X in *A. X B*: if X alone states the relation between A and B (*Earlier*, *As a
  result*, *Rather* = B is the preferred alternative), it is textual. Phrases that relate part
  of B to the rest of B (*For this reason*, *Because of what he did*, *Like these people*,
  *Without knowing about my feelings*) are marked topical Themes. Calling every linker-like word
  textual is the most common tagging error. (S6, S7)
- **Interpersonal = the writer’s own stance.** *I believe that love is special*: *I believe that*
  is interpersonal; *She believes that…*: *She* is topical (another person’s view is reported
  content); *I believe in love*: *I* is topical (believing is the process). An -ly word up front
  is interpersonal only when it voices the writer’s attitude (*Obviously, he left*); *Hesitantly,
  he left* gives the manner, a topical Theme. *Indeed* and *in fact* can be textual and
  interpersonal at once. (S6, S7, S8)
- **Theme extent: the movement test.** A part that can move elsewhere without changing the
  message stands outside the Theme (*At Tim Russert Children’s Garden, … Friday, …*: the
  location phrase is the Theme, *Friday* can move). Long topical Themes keep their modifiers
  (*Bertrand Russell, a political activist…*). (S6)
- **Colon and semicolon act as textual Themes.** *A: B* signals amplification (≈ *specifically*)
  or an emphatic quotation; *A; B* joins closely related clauses whose relation the reader must
  infer (*The rice crop failed; the cotton did quite well* ≈ *however*). A semicolon also
  separates list items that contain commas. Many colons read over-formal. (S8)
- **Analysis procedure.** Work inside one paragraph at a time. Split into independent units
  (anything that can stand as a sentence; a main clause with its subordinate clauses stays one
  unit). Tag each transition in the fixed priority RT → TT → ERT → ETT → N, searching back from
  the nearest sentence to the paragraph’s first unit. A textual Theme in a paragraph’s first
  sentence has nothing inside the paragraph to link to. Tag links only from what is on the page:
  the result must be the same whatever the analyst’s culture. (S6, S7, S8)
- **Motivated and unmotivated N.** A new Theme the reader can still link with effort is motivated N;
  a paragraph of constant N transitions is exhausting and typical of Vietnamese prose, which
  circles one topic with a string of new Themes (*Ông giám đốc… Người công nhân… Nông dân…*).
  Unmotivated new Themes are normal in conversation and wrong in writing. (S6, S7)
- **Hidden ideas between sentences.** Vietnamese prose often links through a whole unstated idea
  (*it takes a lifetime of study, [but it is worth it, because] the results benefit humanity*).
  English readers cannot recover it; *và* states no relation. Write the idea. (S7)
- **Which repetition natives use.** In the instructor’s data, lexical repetition peaks in
  Vietnamese writing Vietnamese, referential repetition in Americans writing English, and
  textual repetition (connectors) in Vietnamese writing English, because schools teach “add
  connectors to make a paragraph coherent”. A linear chain links tail to head with few
  connectors. When grading, check that every textual Theme states the actual relation: in the
  kids paragraph, *Besides* hides a break (S3 does not develop S2), which is exactly where the
  re-chaining found the gap. (S6)
- **Over-linking.** Adding a textual Theme where lexical or referential repetition already links
  is justified only for deliberate emphasis, such as a turn in the argument (*But if the taxpayer
  argument held…*). (S6, S8)
- **The head decides the next Theme.** *I miss the garden of my house. In the garden there are many
  trees* flows; *I miss the lake beside the garden of my house. In the garden…* breaks, because
  the head is *lake*. With *the school and its graduation rate* (equal weight), take one first
  (RT), then return to the other (ERT). Summary nouns pick up whole verb phrases (*This argument*,
  *A draconian measure such as this*). (S8)
- **Set realistic targets.** Expert prose breaks RT deliberately; most transitions RT is enough.
  RT is necessary for linearity and insufficient alone: RT links can be laid over a chain that
  loops. Chain first, TP second: TP work done before the chain is fixed is wasted. (S8)
- **Native editing confirms RT.** A native editor of an in-flight magazine split a long Vietnamese
  sentence into two and moved the company name into the first Rheme, so the second sentence
  continues RT (*Passengers flying through Ho Chi Minh City… A subsidiary of Vietnam Airlines,
  TIAGS is committed to…*). (S6)
- **Writing Vietnamese.** Apply linearity to Vietnamese texts, but keep Vietnamese TP habits: forced
  RT in Vietnamese sounds childish (*mẹ đi chợ, chợ rất gần, gần là không xa…*). (S7)
- **Soup and skewer.** Vietnamese argumentative prose has been likened to a bowl of watery soup
  with ideas floating in it (Hoàng Ngọc Tuấn and Nguyễn Hưng Quốc); English is a skewer, every
  piece on one line in order. English is writer-responsible: an examiner cannot ask what you
  meant. (S8)
- **Exemplification drives TP.** Two long, detailed stories keep RT easily; seven short examples
  about different people force N and spin around the topic. In exams, use one or two specific,
  developed examples, and tell a rationale (*if parents lead a healthy life…*) from a real case
  (*our neighbours’ children…*). (S8, S36)

## 4. The Vietnamese TP problems and reference (→ `02`, `03`)

- **Marked Themes without purpose.** In the instructor’s data Vietnamese writers of English use
  marked topical Themes as often as Americans, but the Vietnamese way, without a purpose, which
  breaks RT for nothing. Front *In Vietnam* only when Vietnam is the exception. Self-audit: list
  every marked Theme in your old essays and ask whether it breaks RT and what it is for; if the
  only answer is Vietnamese word order, unmark it. Used the native way, a marked Theme links:
  *…responsible investigation of their possibilities. To that end, their projects should be
  funded…* (S9)
- **Repair minimally, rank repairs by speed of uptake.** When another writer’s chain is fine,
  keep their order and change as little as possible; one well-chosen marked Theme can fix two
  transitions (*To this cause, thousands of Vietnamese soldiers…*). Ranked by how fast readers get
  the link: *To take advantage of this ability, parents…* > *Given this ability, parents…* >
  *Therefore, parents…* (the reader must work out what *therefore* covers). A good reorder secures
  the link forward as well as back (*We decided to use the FPGA board* puts the board in Rheme for
  the next sentence). (S9)
- **Theme shift and voice.** *…a lot of birds around the campus, and students often feed them* →
  *…a lot of birds around the campus, which are often fed by students* (main point: many birds).
  Where the passive would shift voice awkwardly, a lexical repair is cleaner (*it gained few
  supporters but many more opponents*). The style-guide rule “never make new information the
  subject of a passive” is one of dozens of passive rules; TP explains it in one stroke: that
  passive creates a sudden Theme shift. Natives understand small shifts, but a whole essay of them
  reads non-native, so fix even the simple ones. (S9)
- **Cross-linking.** A sentence that alternates referents (*the difference… my companions… it…
  they*) crosses its links. Finish one referent, then move to the next: *Although the difference
  was not much, it did surprise my companions, who had never experienced anything similar…* (S9)
- **Perspective shift.** *The Mongolian army… lost three times in its invasions of Vietnam. This
  victory…* jumps from the loser’s view to the winner’s; signal it (*This victory by the
  Vietnamese people…*). Names carry perspective too: *the Vietnam War* (American usage), *the
  American War* (Vietnamese usage); for mixed readers choose a neutral form. (S9)
- **Rheme-Rheme redundancy hides behind big words.** Vietnamese writers re-say an idea in new words
  and feel they have added one; colour-coding an English teacher’s paragraph showed one idea (no
  evidence = ill-founded = invalid) said three times in rich vocabulary. Ask of every sentence:
  a new idea, or the same road in new words? Strip the restatements from a 600-word test essay and
  little may remain. Fronting the shared Rheme fixes both problems (*This would have been
  impossible several decades ago*). (S9)
- **Reference shift.** *children… they… As a child, he… children can…* changes number for no reason;
  Vietnamese writers find the switch “varied”, English readers cannot see why it happens. Pick one
  form and hold it (the instructor prefers the singular when a plural *they* would blur *he and
  his friends*). Person shift reads badly too: *mỗi người… chúng ta… các bạn cần…*, rendered in
  English, first includes everyone and then exempts the writer. (S9)
- **Singular they.** The course advised caution in exams, where a marker may count it as an error.
  Current style manuals endorse it: APA Style (7th ed., §4.18) and *The Chicago Manual of Style*
  (18th ed., 2024, ¶5.51) for a person whose gender is unknown or unstated. Use it in research and
  application writing; in a timed language test, *he or she* or a plural rewrite avoids the risk.
  (S9)
- **Cohesion and coherence, made concrete.** *Any country music lover would know Ring of Fire as the
  most famous hit of Johnny Cash’s career, a country music star of the 60s* is coherent (everyone
  understands it) and fails cohesion (the appositive attaches to *career*). Many wrong options in
  SAT, GMAT and GRE items are coherent yet uncohesive. (S9)
- **Reference choices.** Limit cataphora; when you use it (*Because his drink was cold, David
  ordered another cup*), have a reason and keep pointer and referent in one clause. Accessibility
  rises from *the* to *this/that* to *it*: native writers use *this/that* for a whole situation and
  *it* for one concrete item in focus. *This/that* also encode time and the speaker’s position
  (*that deal* fell through; *That night I cooked this romantic dinner*). Cataphora to a set
  defined by what follows takes *that/those* only (*There are those who say…*). Exophoric
  reference (*ấy*; *the bar* for the bar exam) fails readers outside the culture; speech can point,
  writing cannot. (S10)
- **Ellipsis and substitution.** To check a surface form, write the full sentence with nothing
  replaced or deleted, then delete repeats from near to far. Keep the dummy *do* where deletion
  creates two readings (`I like cheese more than Yvette does`). Fossilised ellipses become
  connectors: *To illustrate this point* (topical Theme) → *To illustrate,* (textual). (S10)
- **Lexical cohesion and culture.** Readers link *cats… dogs… Animals* through the category, but TP
  analysis counts only explicit links, because lexical links depend on culture. So strong native
  writers who link lexically show more N Themes than their prose deserves, and Theme-Rheme
  analysis (strict, rule-based) is narrower than topic-comment analysis (looser, used by popular
  IELTS guides). After `not only [warm personality] but also…` readers expect a parallel quality;
  *a variety of animals* breaks the lexical link. (S10)
- **Keep links inside the paragraph.** *To support their petition* (cataphoric, to the officials
  named next) beats *the petition*, which would send the reader back into the previous paragraph.
  Never make an examiner look back across paragraphs; if you would have to, link forward.
  (S10)
- **Word-level linearity.** *…foreign tourists, who have enjoyed other better services in Asian
  countries* → *…better services in other Asian countries*: *other* is redundant before *better*
  (the later word contains the earlier one) and missing before *Asian countries* (Vietnam is
  Asian). One word can also move the reader inside a set: *There are tons of other freaks here*.
  (S10)

## 5. Cohesive devices and conjunctive relations (→ `03`, `linking-items.md`)

- **Weigh every connective.** Speech runs online, so speakers add *tuy… nhưng* for fluency
  (*người dân tuy còn nghèo nhưng rất hiền hậu* presupposes that poor people are normally unkind).
  Writing can be checked: weigh the contrast meaning of every *however*. When explaining an
  error, say first why the device fails, then offer the fix. (S11)
- **Translations hide the relation.** Vietnamese *thay vì* covers contrast, exclusion and
  alternative. *In contrast* sets two coexisting facts against each other; *rather* and
  *instead* exclude one option; *alternatively* leaves both open (train or coach to Hải Phòng).
  *On the contrary* encodes the speaker’s view that the two cannot co-occur. Learn connectives
  through their relations; a Vietnamese gloss hides them. (S1, S10, S11)
- **When and while.** *When* marks a point or a precondition (*I’ll think about it when it’s time
  to write the report*); *while* marks a period, simultaneity or contrast; contrast takes only
  *while* or its formal partner `whereas`; *whilst* is formal and old-fashioned. *When he closed the door, she…* is
  sequence; *While he closed the door…* is simultaneity. (S11)
- **Choose the narrowest connective.** *Moreover*, *furthermore* and *and* fit almost any pair, so
  they clarify nothing; when *similarly* or *likewise* fits, use it. Narrow choices also vary the
  prose. (S11)
- **Two conditions for on the one hand / on the other hand.** The pair needs contrast and
  parallelism; *however* needs contrast only. Check by finding the parallel element, then the
  contrast. *On the other hand* can stand without *on the one hand*. (S11)
- **A shared item is no relation.** If two halves share only a word (*mathematics*) and no real
  relation, *while* is wrong; join them through that item with a relative clause (*…mathematics,
  the cornerstone of which…*). (S11)
- **Lexical conditions on connectives.** `instead of` and *apart from* need members of one lexical
  category (`Dogs deserve to be our best friends instead of food` fails; the real relation is
  cause: *…and hence should not be killed for food*). *Apart from* needs no overlap between A and
  B; *despite* marks an obstacle and needs no shared category. *Let alone* needs one set with B
  the more extreme case (*I can’t walk, let alone run*) and repeats the verb form of its partner,
  since it works by ellipsis. (S11)
- **Position shows scope.** A sentence-initial *However* links to the previous sentence; to
  contrast two parts of one sentence, place it inside (*After years in vogue, ALM, however, is
  challenged…*). (S11)
- **Long-distance links: build A′.** When B must link to an A far above, put a paraphrase A′ next to
  B (*No matter how important marks are to students, they should not be used as an absolute
  criterion…*), the way a partner abroad becomes a video call. Signalled repetition (*in other
  words*) and A′ are the two permitted kinds of repetition. Speakers do the same (*What happened
  between you and Mum?*). (S11)
- **The cardinality test.** Cause and effect are many-to-many; paraphrase is one-to-one (necessary
  and sufficient both ways); *instead* is one-to-many. *She scored below 7 because she did not
  score 7 or above* is paraphrase posing as cause; Vietnamese *cho nên* hides the difference.
  Combination and amplification differ by dependency (equal weight vs a main idea plus a minor
  addition); sequence is no proof of cause. (S11, S12)
- **The second component weighs more.** In cause-effect pairs the second member carries the
  weight (*A because B* stresses the cause; *A, so B* the effect): pick the connective that puts
  your main point second. Contrast works the same way: *You’re a good student but you’ve made a
  lot of mistakes* is criticism, and *capable yet old people* says the reverse of *old yet
  capable people*. When this conflicts with subordination (*although*), the dependency rules in
  `04` decide. (S12)
- **And or or.** Vietnamese barely distinguishes them in lists. *…where no one would overcharge you,
  rip you off or treat you poorly* (avoid any of these); *two explanations… are that… and that…*
  (both stand together); *a big mistake, a prejudice* (apposition: one kind of the other). (S12)
- **Know the role of the linking word.** *After*, *as*, *both* and *but* each serve as conjunction,
  adverb or preposition (*but* also means *except* and *only*), and each role links different
  parts with different weight. *You are older than I* (conjunction plus ellipsis) and *older
  than me* (preposition) are both correct. (S1, S12)
- **Reconstructing a paragraph.** Find the openers by elimination (sentences with anaphors, specific
  examples and paragraph-internal linkers cannot open), subgroup, order by conjunctions,
  references and lexical cohesion, then check linearity and argument structure. In an
  argumentative paragraph: topic sentence → rationale → specific example; across paragraphs:
  own position → concession → rebuttal → return to the position. *Of course* grants a point
  readily before *but* rebuts; *In some situations, however,* frames the opposing view as valid
  only in limited cases. (S12)
- **Why capable writers still err.** Two causes: no systematic knowledge of English relations, and
  implicit links (the connective joins a written idea to an unwritten one). Write RT-heavy while
  linear instinct is weak; lean on lexical cohesion once it is secure, keeping the idea flow
  linear. (S11)

## 6. Dependency and clause types (→ `04`)

- **Tests that settle the grammar.** (1) Conjunction or adverb? Try moving it: an adverb moves
  freely (*It is, however, a decision we have to make*), a conjunction must open its clause.
  (2) Restrictive or not? Split into two sentences: if the first still identifies the referent
  alone, the clause is non-restrictive (*John has a daughter who is a teacher*: several
  daughters; *…a daughter, who is a teacher*: one). *Only* forces the restrictive reading (*Only
  schools that allow…*). (3) Nominal clause? It can move into subject position (*When I travel
  by train is unknown to me*). One linking item can open all three clause types (*the date when
  I travel* / *read a book when I travel* / *don’t know when I travel*). (S13, S14)
- **Coordinators are few.** `and, or, but, for, nor, either/neither, so, yet, plus`; treat every
  other conjunction as subordinating. Coordinators followed by a noun phrase still join clauses
  by ellipsis (`His death was not a tragedy, but a release from suffering`). Sources disagree on
  *so*; between sentences, an adverb (*therefore, as a result, consequently*) avoids the
  question. (S13)
- **Prepositions subordinate their phrase.** In *We had those stupid fights due to lack of sleep* the
  fights are the point; to make lack of sleep the point, rewrite (*Lack of sleep is the reason we
  had those stupid fights*). (S13)
- **Adverb position shows focus.** *They, therefore, should…* stresses *should*; *…allowed to work,
  therefore, longer* stresses *longer* and tells the reader the next sentence is about duration.
  Between two independent clauses in one sentence use a semicolon, or *, and therefore*; a bare
  comma leaves the reader unsure that both clauses stand alone. (S13)
- **Register of cause items.** Neutral *therefore, as a result, accordingly*; formal *thus, hence,
  thereby, consequently*; *ergo* is literary. *As a result* presents one of several effects; for
  the only result, natives prefer *The result is…*. *With the result that* is formal, old and
  falling in use. *Given that* [A] and *on the grounds that* [LW] outrank *because* in register;
  *for* is literary. A free-standing *Because…* sentence is speech; in writing it is a fragment
  and costs marks. *Thereby* marks an immediate consequence; the claim that it can never join two
  sentences has no support in corpus data. When unsure about a form, check a standard corpus.
  (S13, S14)
- **Commas change meaning.** *Anyone with a fever who has travelled from mainland China in the past
  14 days* (only feverish travellers) and *anyone with a fever, who has travelled…* (any feverish
  person) apply a rule to different people. A comma is a yellow light; long sentences also take
  commas for breath. (S13, S14)
- **Relative clauses differ in tone; all are grammatical.** *The young woman with whom I spent hours talking*
  > *whom I spent hours talking with* > *who…* > *[that] I spent hours talking with*: all
  correct, rising in informality. *Who/which* can become *that* only when no preposition precedes
  it. *What* = *that which* [F, O]. (S14)
- **Reduction and the subject rule.** A non-restrictive clause with an ordinary verb reduces only
  when its subject matches the main subject (*I gave the book to my sister, working as a
  receptionist* is a classic wrong option); with *be* it reduces whatever the subject. Decide
  first what *which* attaches to (in test items, the nearest possible noun). Only *when possible*
  and *when necessary* reduce with a different subject. *In that* opens a nominal clause
  (*different in that she is more attentive*); *in which* opens a relative one. (S14)
- **Absolute and embedded clauses.** Absolute clauses (*The weather being bad, I cannot go out*)
  show level, but many examiners will mark them wrong, so use them with care in tests. An
  embedded clause carries a minor idea without breaking the Theme before it or the Rheme after it
  (*Tom, even though he doesn’t like it, is smiling at her*). Non-finite clauses are easiest to
  handle by restoring their hidden subject. (S14)
- **Say what you mean about certainty.** Vietnamese speakers say *I think* to sound modest; in
  English *I think* and *I know* differ in certainty. If you know, write *I know*. (S14)
- **Test strategy.** In GMAT-style sentence correction it is easier to prove options wrong than one
  right; every item reduces to knowing the full original sentence and each change applied to it.
  Preparing for these tests stretches thinking even for those who never sit them. (S10, S14)

## 7. Linking items: what the lecture glosses add (→ `linking-items.md`, `04`)

Selected glosses from the 40-item reconstruction task (S13 to S18). Each item is chosen in three
steps: the relation between the halves, the grammatical role the slot needs (a comma after the
slot points to an adverb), then an item that satisfies both, checked for register.

- **Contrast.** *Nevertheless* and *nonetheless* [F] need a direct, explicit contrast; *however*
  serves explicit and implicit contrast. Clause-final *though* is an adverb (*I’ll have a cup of
  tea though*); *although* cannot end a clause. *Yet* sounds pompous in casual speech and neutral
  in academic prose. *Albeit* [F, LW] usually takes a reduced phrase (*an amazing computer, albeit
  expensive*). *Despite the weather* still implies bad weather; *regardless of the weather* means
  whatever it is. (S16)
- **Two relations at once.** A conjunction can join an adverb (*and specifically*, *yet at the same
  time*, *and yet*), which adds a second relation; two conjunctions cannot stack. *Specifically*
  adds detail, and the detail may run long without becoming the main point: importance and length
  are different things. (S16)
- **Dialogue.** *A: The meeting was terrible. B: In contrast / On the contrary, I enjoyed it.* Both
  work, depending on the inference B draws (A did not enjoy it, or A assumed B did not). (S16)
- **Exception and replacement.** *But* = *except* only after *all, every-, any-, no-* (*Everyone but
  Richard was late*). *Except* stresses the exception; *except for* stresses that the statement is
  incomplete. Negation flips amplification into exclusion (*Several others know besides her* /
  *No one knows besides her*). `Rather than` states a preference between two options; *as opposed
  to* contrasts strongly. (S16, S17)
- **Sequence and time.** *Later on* belongs to speech. Once at a formal level, stay there (*the week
  prior to the meeting* sits with *unfortunate events transpired*). *At first* implies the
  situation no longer holds. *By Friday* is a deadline point; *until Friday* the whole period.
  Contrastive *meanwhile* (*You throw food away, and meanwhile children are starving*) is mainly
  British. Reduced clauses take *while* (*while waiting for the bus*). (S17)
- **Amplification.** *Moreover* is the academic item (far more frequent in academic corpora than in
  newspapers); *furthermore* is rising. *Also / as well / too* need the members to point the same
  way (*…and she’s a bad dancer as well* jars); so does *not to mention*. *In fact* can confirm or
  contradict (*It may look simple. In fact, it’s very difficult*); *indeed* only confirms.
  *Plus* as a conjunction is spoken. British dictionaries give *on top of* for negative additions
  only. (S17)
- **Exemplification and specification.** *Like* meaning “for example” is spoken; *like* meaning
  “similar to” is fine in writing. *For example* gives an instance; *specifically* adds detail;
  used together, *for example* comes first. *Namely* must list every member; *viz.* is fading and
  sounds elderly. *Especially* singles out a case; *specially* means for a particular purpose.
  *Insofar as* is rising, *inasmuch as* falling. (S17)
- **Correlatives work by factoring out.** As in algebra (AC + BC → (A + B)C): *Both she and her sister
  live in New Orleans* (the verb changes form, so this is factoring out, beyond plain ellipsis).
  Place the adverb *either* just before the partner of the *or* member; in `not only… but also`,
  *but* is a conjunction and stays put while *also* may move. Vietnamese repetition of a whole
  predicate for each party (workers, employers and the state each pay 1%) factors out the same
  way: (A + B + C) → D. (S17, S18)
- **Condition, precaution, counterfactual.** *Unless we hurry* = *We must hurry; otherwise / or else…*.
  *As long as* is the most informal of *providing, provided, as long as*. *In case* stresses
  possibility (indicative); *lest* [F, O] stresses worry and takes the subjunctive (*lest a thief
  sneak in*). `If it were not for / were it not for / but for` [F]; `if it was not for` is common
  but non-standard for a counterfactual. (S18)
- **Mood and distance.** *If it were me, I would…* (subjunctive: formal, distant, polite) and *If it
  was me…* (conditional: casual, close) mean the same. *I wish I were* treats *wish* as a
  hypothetical frame; *I wish I am* uses *wish* as a plain verb of wanting. (S18)
- **Settle disputes with data.** When dictionaries disagree (*so*; `me neither` vs `me either`;
  *as a result* vs *as the result*), check frequencies in a standard corpus or Ngram, and learn
  the logic under the rule (*Everybody but I has paid* sounds formal because *but* was once a
  conjunction meaning “except”). (S13, S17)

## 8. Linking scope, syntax, distance and explicitness (→ `05`)

- **Why trees.** Syntax trees make linking scope visible, explain word order with logic, and train
  critical thinking the way algebra does (*a + b × c* vs *(a + b) × c*). Draw categories (PP, NP),
  start from the head, attach right before left and near before far, and keep testing higher
  nodes: each node that works adds a reading. Flat diagrams that put everything on one level hide
  both scope and dependency. Coordinated clauses take a three-way branch; a subordinate clause
  sits one level lower. (S18, S20, S22)
- **Complement and adjunct.** A complement is obligatory, sits next to its head in the same scope
  and keeps a fixed position (*always kiss Kate quickly* is fine; *always kiss quickly Kate* drops
  an adjunct between head and complement). Adjuncts are optional, stack, and move within their own
  clause; moving one into a higher clause changes its scope (*Frequently Kate knows that Andy
  eats sushi* makes the knowing frequent). The same contrast explains agreement (*California,
  along with Florida and Hawaii, is…*: the movable *along with* phrase is an adjunct), negative
  inversion and tag questions. Phrasal verbs (*look new words up*) differ from prepositional verbs
  (*look up her skirt*) by the same test. (S20, S23)
- **T carries the grammar.** Tense, aspect, voice, negation, modality, *to* and *-ing* sit in T, in
  the order modal/tense → negation → aspect → voice → verb. English has no question particle, so
  T moves up to signal a question, and when T has no sound of its own, dummy *do* carries it; the
  same *do* carries negation, ellipsis (*He did*) and emphasis (*I do love you*). Tense relates the
  reference point to the time of speaking; aspect relates the event to the reference point. The
  present perfect is the one form that also covers an action still in contact with now. (S22,
  S23)
- **One reading only.** A sentence that allows two readings must be rewritten: *came to Vietnam early
  in January* (ahead of schedule) vs *in early January*; *not persuasive and unreasonable* (the
  negation may cover both) → *unpersuasive and unreasonable*; *to manage their time better and
  balance study and fun* → *to better manage their time and balance…*; *how badly people treated
  him and stole money from him* → *how people treated him badly and stole…* (stealing already
  carries the judgement). Swapping two words changes scope: *contribute more substantially* (in
  quality) vs *contribute substantially more* (in quantity); *paint a door green* vs *paint a
  green door*. (S22, S23)
- **Keep *that*.** Dropping *that* creates garden paths (*The author wrote the novel was a big
  success*) and attachment doubts (*You complain in Vietnam you get overcharged*). In exam writing
  keep it every time; repeating it (*agree that X but that Y*) keeps both claims inside the
  scope of *agree*. (S22)
- **Commas set scope.** A comma that opens an insertion (*These products, namely…*) must be closed by
  a second comma. A full stop closes a conditional space: after *If advertising did not exist, new
  concepts could never reach us.* a new sentence beginning *Thus we would…* has left the
  hypothetical world. (S22, S24)
- **Watch the hidden subject.** An infinitive or participle needs an NP to supply its doer; *The
  living standard in Vietnam has stayed too low to be claimed as a meat-eater country* links the
  doer to *the living standard*. In test items, a fronted participial clause (*Responding to…*)
  must attach to the subject that performs it. (S23)
- **Linking distance.** Minimise the total distance across all linked pairs. Bring compared items
  together (*parents, in comparison with friends, influence younger daughters more*). If A links
  to both B and C, place A between them: *treatment of a disease common in the elderly*; *one of
  the regions most prone to flooding*; an adjective with its own complement follows the noun.
  Interleaved pairs (A-B-C-D with A linked to C and B to D) cross; reorder to A-C-B-D. (S23)
- **Cross-paragraph links.** Opening a paragraph with *The first group of people…* sends readers
  back to the previous paragraph. Keep such links rare, and where one is unavoidable place a copy
  of the antecedent (A′) beside it. When A branches into B and C, develop B fully, then reopen C
  with C′. Building A′ and C′ is a main use of paraphrase. (S11, S24)
- **Native writers err too.** Advertisements and headlines show the same faults (*Specialist in women
  and other diseases*; *eats anything, loves children*); a reported speaker’s further words must
  stay attributed (*Cụ thể hơn…* belongs to the person quoted). In a CV line, *freelance* attaches
  to the work, *monthly* to the publication. (S23, S24)
- **Explicitness: three causes of implicit links.** (1) Writers rely on links they understand
  themselves (*The rice crop failed. The cotton did quite well*: contrast or compensation?). (2)
  They assume a shared schema: readers of an essay on Hồ Chí Minh need to be told that the young
  Nguyễn Tất Thành is the same person; a child-labour anecdote (*started work at 14, now looks 90*)
  evokes poverty for Vietnamese readers and ageing for an American one. (3) They expect readers to
  infer (*Family and friends are both important, each can have an influence…*: cause, or
  contrast?). Examiners will not puzzle links out; they mark them down. (S1, S24)
- **Supply schema.** Whenever the reader may lack background, add it in passing: *on VTV3, the most
  watched TV channel in Vietnam*; what Đồng Nai is; hectares converted for readers who do not use
  them; a scandal named after a TV character explained through the actor and the series. A passage
  about doing laundry stays opaque until its topic is given; jokes play rival schemas against
  each other. (S24)
- **Unpack, then convert metaphors.** *His sister won a scholarship. Thanks to that, my boyfriend
  always tries hard* runs through an unstated idea; write it (*Not to be outdone…*). A hidden
  premise loosens the argument, because a reader who rejects it rejects the claim. A
  word-for-word proverb (*Those who keep at working iron will eventually make needles*) reads as
  a riddle: convert it to an English equivalent (*Practice makes perfect*) and unpack the link to
  the next sentence. Start by over-explaining, then trim; readers who cannot follow tend to
  over-read (the “blue curtains” meme). (S24)

## 9. Economy of language (→ `08`)

- **Speech and writing differ.** In speaking, some redundancy keeps fluency and buys thinking time;
  in writing, the leaner the better. *In my opinion, I agree* and *From my perspective, I
  believe* stack two markers of one view; IELTS books model such phrases for speaking. (S25)
- **Small redundancies.** *seem to be so busy* → *seem so busy*; *after having watched* → *having
  watched*; *more additional evidence*, *collaborate together*, *most favourite* (Vietnamese
  “thêm… bổ sung”, “hợp tác với nhau”); *Some of the potential health risks include… etc.* →
  *Potential health risks include…* (*include* already implies a partial list); *He said that he
  would sing* → *He promised to sing*. (S25)
- **Default values.** Leave out what readers assume: *simply by saving his money* → *simply by
  saving*; Vietnamese *có* produces *There are many kids in Vietnam who don’t have a chance* →
  *Many kids in Vietnam don’t have a chance*. (S25)
- **Find the compact word.** Think of the compact Vietnamese word first (*phản tác dụng*), then its
  English equivalent (*counterproductive*); build formal words by affixation in an exam
  (*repair → repairable → irreparable*); fold a clause into a modifier (*a region where most
  residents are farmers* → *an agrocentric region*); name the established term
  (*anecdotal sources*, which also signals research literacy when rebutting an opponent who
  relies on personal observation). Use an idiom only when sure of its form and register. (S25)
- **The noun phrase principle matters most.** Condensing a clause or a long phrase into an NP makes
  prose shorter, more formal and better linked (*the evaluation at the end of the year* → *the
  year-end evaluation*). When A links to two unlike elements, turn one into a modifier of A (*The
  slow-growing labour pool is limited*), choosing by dependency. (S25)
- **Word limits keep shrinking.** Essays once allowed 2,000 words; 500 or 250 is now typical, and
  some schemes count characters. Conveying most in fewest words shows ability and spares a small
  committee reading thousands of files. Writing 100 words may take a day; cutting takes longer.
  (S29)
- **What to cut first in application essays.** (1) Boilerplate openings and closings (*I am writing
  this application to be considered for…*; pledges to make the programme proud). (2) Hollow
  sentences: if a sentence would fit any applicant (*I want to experience the culture, people and
  language*), delete it. (3) Long context before the main story: compress it to a line or two.
  (4) Literary flourishes, which say nothing about the applicant. (5) Explanations of what every
  applicant does (*I took the TOEFL and GMAT, the latter making me think more critically*). Never
  cut specific figures (a hire who raised revenue by 28%). (S29)

## 10. Modality (→ `07`)

- **Why Vietnamese writers misuse modals.** In the instructor’s corpus figures (as given in class),
  Vietnamese writers use CAN about 26 times as often as COULD (Americans about 2.5 times) and WILL
  about 2.5 times as often as WOULD (Americans use them about equally), with SHOULD and MUST at
  nearly twice the American rate: an imposing tone. The cause is translation (*có thể* → *can*,
  *sẽ* → *will*, *nên* → *should*). (S1)
- **Why the force model.** Function lists blur (is *Mẹ cho con lấy chồng đi* permission or a
  request?) and say nothing about prediction. Forces explain every choice: *suggest < advise <
  demand < command* share one meaning and differ in strength. Past forms are weaker because the
  past encodes distance: close by, I can act myself; at a distance only gentle words work. The
  instructor added a third role (force coordinator = the speaker who surveys the forces) to the
  two-role model of the source account, which could not explain every case. (S24, S26)
- **Decide social or logical first.** Almost every modal has both readings, and mixing them is the
  commonest error in gap-fill items (*You must be Leonard’s mother*, answered *I don’t know if I must be,
  but yes*; *Could you show me…?*, answered *Of course I could*). Then analyse the forces. For simple social
  cases the traditional shortcuts still work (advice → *should*, an order → *must*). (S26, S27,
  S28)
- **Social strength.** Nothing modal is stronger than social *will* (*You will submit your paper
  today*): the speaker’s own will at full strength, leaving only compliance; never use it upwards
  (to a professor or a boss). *Must* is an irresistible external force (the rules, out of the
  speaker’s hands), so it is weaker than *will* socially. *Going to* reports a prior plan (*I’m
  gonna kill you*: the wish has become a plan), *will* a wish arising now. (S26, S27)
- **Prediction rests on a basis.** *Will* and *must* differ in source; their certainty is similar: *That will be
  Tom* rests on intuition (persuades poorly without data, so it is rare in academic argument);
  *That must be Tom* rests on evidence you can list. *Should* extrapolates a past pattern (he came
  at 8 the last two evenings); *would* rests on personal experience or putting oneself in
  another’s place; *could* weighs evidence for and against (Tom or Andy might be knocking);
  *may/might* carry no force (50–50). More evidence moves the modal up: a woman with a child
  *should* be married (pattern); add a wedding ring and a waving husband and she *must* be. (S26,
  S27, S28)
- **Should lectures.** Vietnamese soften *phải* to *nên* and write *should*, but *should* carries
  moral force; essays full of *should* make readers feel lectured, a question an American reader once
  put to the instructor. Requirements read the same way: *Candidates should have a
  master’s degree and must be fluent in English* means the master’s is a past pattern among
  winners (apply without one if you must), while fluency is mandatory. *Ought to* is falling and
  sounds needlessly formal; *supposed to* reports an expectation imposed by others. (S27)
- **Could and may/might in argument.** *Could* is the modal of alternatives: each option is the drag
  on the other (*His claim… could be either a deliberate exaggeration or simple ignorance*;
  *Hillary Clinton, who could soon become…*). In a recommendation letter, *his qualities could make
  him a good researcher* makes native readers ask what the drag is: write *make*. Use *may/might*
  to concede the opponent’s points; on your own position they would signal weak
  confidence; *This point could be illustrated by…* steers readers, *might* leaves them free to
  disagree. (S27, S28)
- **Shall** keeps external authority and binding obligation in legal text (*The Ministry of Finance
  shall regulate…*: assigned, no choice; *It shall be unlawful to carry firearms*) and fixed
  formulas (*Shall we dance?*); elsewhere *will* has replaced it. (S27)
- **Tests and timetables.** For a prediction in an essay prompt (*Children in a big city ___ have
  better access to high-quality education because cities have good schools…*), *will* is too
  certain for thin evidence. In exams pick *may* for social uses and *might* for logical ones, the
  safe split. *Could possibly* and *can possibly* are redundant in writing. The simple present
  states a timetable as a present fact (*The train leaves at 19:45*); *The train will have left by
  now* is logical *will* plus perfect aspect (a near-certain inference about the past). (S26, S28)
- **Perfect modals.** First decide: guessing what happened (logical: every modal can take part:
  *He might have missed the train*; *They should have finished by seven; the other teams all did*)
  or knowing it did not happen and wishing otherwise (social: only internal-force modals, *should*
  and *would*: *You should have studied harder*), or possible then, impossible now (*I could have
  bought the house for $50,000*). This settles contradictions between dictionaries and teaching
  videos that only rank certainty. (S30)
- **Standard grammars on two course claims.** The course treats CAN as lacking a logical use and
  reads *can’t* as asserting impossibility. Standard grammars (Quirk et al., 1985; Biber et al.,
  1999; Huddleston & Pullum, 2002) class *That can’t be Tom* and *She can’t have seen it* as
  epistemic: logical impossibility, the negative counterpart of *must*. In research prose use
  *cannot* for a logical exclusion and keep *can* for ability and general possibility. The course
  contrasts *must* and *have to* by force source; corpus studies report that deontic *must* has
  declined in both British and American English while *have to* and *need to* rose (Leech et
  al., 2009). (S27, S28)

## 11. Qualification, vocabulary and political correctness (→ `07` B, `10`)

- **Qualify inferences; leave facts unhedged.** An inference cannot assert certainty (*As inferred
  from the article, you conclude…* → *you apparently assume…*); a fact needs no hedge (*You could
  forget to mention that China attempted to invade…* → *You fail to mention…*). A rationale (an
  assumption in your head) needs careful qualification; a specific example (something observed)
  does not. (S32)
- **Close the door on the objector.** The main purpose of qualification is to shut out the reader
  who would object: *three differences* collapses when someone names a fourth; *at least three
  lifestyle differences* survives. Narrow or widen to fit: *the worst show I have ever watched*;
  *one of the worst shows ever*. A writer who redefines a term the opponent used and then refutes
  the redefinition has refuted nothing (a straw man by definition). (S32)
- **Expert writers stack qualifiers.** *Hugo Schwyzer might seem like just the kind of professor
  who would like this website* holds five qualifying elements in one clause; Vietnamese does not
  stack them, so learners must build the habit consciously. In Anglo-American academic culture,
  careful qualification signals civility and opens space for the reader. Mark an absolute claim
  explicitly with *unqualified* (*unqualified support*). Spoken qualifiers (*kind of*, *sort of*,
  *as far as I know*) belong in chatty writing only. (S32)
- **Qualification errors in learners’ drafts.** Redundancy (*Most of our discussions are usually
  productive*), logical contradiction, and a qualifier that reaches the wrong scope. Gradable
  adjectives take hedges; non-gradable ones do not (*This example is not true to some extent*).
  (S32)
- **Academic words live in narrow contexts.** Learn each in context and use it only there (*a tough
  proposition*; *ready access to quality food*; *constitute a good source of*). When the English
  will not come, ask how an educated Vietnamese would say it formally (*nhìn thấy* → *quan sát* →
  *observe*). *Apparently* means *seemingly* (most students read it as *clearly*); the adjective
  *apparent* keeps both senses. Word substitution is the easiest layer of academic style. (S31)
- **Shift the register, beyond swapping words.** A child’s *my dad kills bugs for a living* becomes *pest
  control specialist*; phrase every CV role and duty at that level. The share of academic
  vocabulary rises with level (the instructor’s rough figures: secondary school 7–8%,
  undergraduate 9–10%, graduate 11–12%; an IELTS band-9 model essay scored about 8%), so IELTS
  writing does not prepare students for graduate prose. Treat these figures as rough guides;
  rates vary by field. (S31)
- **Political correctness.** Phrase sensitive, negative matters as politely as possible, often
  indirectly and in the negative: *I had to go to a bad school* → *I did not have the opportunity
  to attend a better school*; *I don’t think we should* over *I think we shouldn’t*; *a boring
  teacher* → *an uninspiring teacher*. A form asking *In what areas could the applicant improve?*
  asks for weaknesses. Tone matters as much as words. For current terms (income groups; “developing
  countries” is outdated) follow `10` and `13` §10. (S31)

## 12. Applications: what the lectures and live reviews add (→ `annwuyn-scholarship`)

### Planning and the CV (S15, S19, L7)

- **Start in the first or second year of university.** GPA, research, teaching and referee
  relationships take years. The target degree decides the funding route: PhD funding usually
  comes from the university (research or teaching assistantships); master’s applicants rely
  mostly on external scholarships, TA posts (teaching experience) or RA posts (research
  experience). (S15)
- **Low GPA, major switching, gaps.** Ask the school to certify your class rank; take conversion
  courses and apply straight to a master’s in the new field (a second bachelor’s abroad wastes
  four or five years); explain every gap consistently with your path, since one or two years at
  home “preparing” with nothing to report is a red flag committees probe. Anticipate every
  question and rehearse the answer. (S15)
- **Show a movie of your path.** Vietnamese applicants present who they are today (degree, GPA, years
  of work); Western readers want the trajectory from the starting point to now and where it
  leads. (S15)
- **The CV signals direction.** A reader who skips the objective line should still infer the goal
  from the degrees, roles and activities. Describe each role through the work that matches the
  goal, with its true scale (*analysed data on 4,000 employees to design the salary policy*, if
  that is what you did), and give research experience its own section. Leave out gender, date of
  birth and marital status; use an institutional email. Report test scores with percentile
  ranks. For skills, name the experiences that built them; never define the skill. (S15, S19, L7)
- **Upward progression.** List duties so the path rises (*teaching → materials and lesson plans →
  curriculum and textbooks*); a later job with a narrower role can be framed as adding practice to
  theory. Quantify against peers (*sales staff average three contracts a year worth about $50,000;
  I signed five worth $200,000*). (S15, S19)

### SOP, PS and essay sets (S19, S21, S29, L1 to L7)

- **One master SOP, tailored each time.** Write one statement covering your whole path, then cut
  and customise it per scheme. A clear direction makes the SOP almost write itself; a long
  struggle shows you do not yet know what you want. You find a sense of purpose by doing the
  work; imagining it is no substitute. (S19)
- **The problem-led spine.** Start from a problem in your field or country, the challenges inside it,
  the part you will contribute to and the experience that qualifies you, what you still lack,
  how this programme supplies it, and what you will do afterwards. One right-sized goal; drop
  goals that compete. Do not force your story into someone else’s chronology. (L1, L6)
- **Process over product.** The CV is the product; the SOP tells the process: problem
  identification → obstacles → solutions → results in perspective → lessons. Failures matter
  because readers want to see how you overcame them. Vague name-dropping (*discussions with famous
  professors who gave valuable advice*) needs the actual advice and its effect. (S19)
- **Openings.** A relevant hook beats a decorative one (*On my 19th birthday, on top of Fansipan…*
  needs a link to the goal); no story beats a weak one; skip famous sayings (in Asian eyes a
  quotation shows learning; to Western readers it shows a borrowed voice). At 500 words or
  fewer, go straight to what the prompt asks. (S19, S29)
- **Goals.** Why study abroad: write what the programme offers that you need; never write that
  Vietnamese education is poor (it describes you too). Master’s vs PhD: a master’s applies what
  others created; a PhD cuts a new path. Why now: argue from the problems you meet in your work.
  (S19)
- **Why this country.** Clichés fit every country (*a developed country*). Dig into its history for
  a problem it met and solved that matches yours in Vietnam, or name a specific institution,
  professor, practice or company there; vital for government-funded schemes. Say what you will
  learn there; claiming you will improve the host country’s society reads as bombast. (S19, L6,
  L7)
- **Avoid stepping-stone signals.** Mention a later degree abroad lightly, after the plan to return
  and apply what you learn; funders want impact at home. (L2)
- **Essay sets are one web.** Short prompts (150 to 500 words each) cannot carry a whole case
  alone. Give every essay one component of one thread, hand off between them, and cross-refer
  (*as I describe in my career-plan essay*) to save words. Open prompts (*What makes you feel
  alive?*) supply the evidence for the rest. The most common failure is answering each prompt
  precisely while the set never forms one thread. (L3, L5, L6)
- **Leadership and networking essays.** Tell a story of influence that answers why others followed
  you (your visible change, useful advice, an initiative colleagues adopted); a definition of
  leadership wastes the word limit. Link networking to leadership: the network spreads the
  influence. A side project already in progress (an English club for lawyers at work) can carry
  both. (L3, L6)
- **Personal Statements.** A PS shows who you are “for better or for worse”: readers may see flaws
  as long as they see the real person and their potential. A frank, ordinary story beats an
  inflated drama (a student who admitted playing computer games at a careers day, then showed
  how it changed her classmates’ use of time). (S21)
- **Each document stands alone.** Scholarship readers read their own letter first and may never
  open the university’s; restate the essentials and add only why they should choose you. Lead
  with your strongest relevant origin (a specialised IT high school for a data programme), drop
  childhood anecdotes, and explain each career move step by step. (L7)

### Letters of recommendation (S21, S25)

- **Weight grows with prestige.** At top schools everyone past screening tops their class, so what
  others say decides. Many professors outside the US do not know how to write a strong letter;
  the applicant must help by supplying information. Some referees send a questionnaire (how long
  have I known you, what would help me remember you…). (S21)
- **Choose referees who know you.** Titles impress nobody; a principal, dean or vice-minister needs
  an explanation of how they know you. Old or new boss: choose the one who has known your work
  longer, and pre-empt doubt in the letter (*She asked me, and not her current manager of six
  months, because I supervised her work for two years*). (S21, L7)
- **Help the referee keep their own voice.** Give each referee a fact sheet (contexts, dates, stories,
  achievements, what the programme values) so the judgement and voice stay theirs. If a referee
  insists that you draft, write one coherent draft per referee from your facts (sample letters
  stitched together read as two writers), keep the three letters distinct in angle and style, and ask the referee to
  revise it into their own words; letters that share one writer’s phrasing or the applicant’s
  SOP sentences are detected. (S21, S25)
- **Make the applicant stand out.** One or two stories for the qualities most relevant to this
  referee’s context; the three letters give three angles. Ask a reviewer: what do I remember
  about this person? Is every compliment backed by evidence? Does the writer visibly care? A
  long-standing problem the applicant solved, and impact on others, count most (“We are looking
  for people who have made and will make an impact”). Put praise in perspective (*he did the
  whole thesis alone while the other projects were done in groups*), and never raise the
  applicant by lowering classmates or the home system. (S21, S25)
- **Endorse without hedges.** *His qualities could make him a good researcher* raises the question
  of what holds him back; write *make*. Weaknesses are framed through progress (*He used to have
  time-management problems*), and a referee can explain a file problem more credibly than the
  applicant (a thesis grade lowered by illness). A real, developmental weakness persuades; a
  disguised boast (“works too hard”) lowers credibility and liking (Sezer et al., 2018).
  (S21, S27)
- **Personal tone.** A letter is personal; a performance report reads cold: *I was very glad
  but not surprised that he…*. Imperfect English from a referee who is not an English teacher is
  credible; correct only errors that could mislead. (S25)

### Professor contact (S33)

- **Professors decide funding.** Departmental faculty nominate the awardees, so a professor who
  wants you nearly secures your place. Prefer junior professors and those with research grants;
  befriend visiting professors at conferences your university hosts; ask the graduate school to
  connect you with current students, who know which professors take students. (S33)
- **The first email is a handshake.** Get to the point (no inquiries after health or weather),
  avoid too much information, have a complete CV ready, and give specific reasons tied to their
  work. Do not open with money; if you already hold external funding, say so at once. Resend
  once after a week, marked *[Second attempt]*, from an institutional address if the first went
  from a free account. Five non-replies are normal. (S33)
- **Later emails.** Put your name in the subject line; ask specific questions answerable in
  reasonable time, one point at a time; never ask what the website or handbook answers, and
  never ask personal questions (salary). Keep them updated on your file; a professor told that
  a cut-off screened you out may intervene. Use title and surname until invited to use the first
  name. At most one email a week; avoid holidays. (S33)

### Package, translation and interview (S38, L1 to L7)

- **Package.** Show care: committees ask who needs the award most as well as who is best, and
  experienced readers see at once whether every sentence was polished or pasted. Numbered parts,
  page headers, initials, a detail-minded checker (another school’s name in an essay is an easy
  slip near deadlines), a cover letter that lists the parts and asks for financial-aid
  consideration. If your university will not issue sealed transcripts, say so in a line and
  invite the school to contact it. A credential evaluation (WES) often raises a Vietnamese GPA,
  because it weights major courses; request it, check the result, then decide where to send it.
  (S38)
- **Translate your own credentials accurately.** Notarise the Vietnamese original and attach your
  own translation; use the official English names of programmes and classifications, add a
  grading explanation, and put achievements in perspective (*one of 10 recipients in a faculty
  of 100*). Name the event as it is called (a contest, a conference) and say what you did there.
  (S38)
- **Why interviews happen.** To confirm that the person matches the file (ghost-written dossiers
  exist), to test passion, knowledge and spoken English, and to probe inconsistencies (low grades
  in the claimed field; a step down between jobs). Reread the file before the interview, since
  months may have passed. (S38)
- **Answering.** Ask for a minute and jot the reasons, then order them; start from the problem; begin
  with the big picture (*describe your working day*: the job’s purposes and how the day serves
  them, before any hour-by-hour detail); show leadership as inspiring others to make a difference;
  for development schemes, say how your work helps the country. Answers point upward: after a
  rejected proposal, describe the pilot it led to and the impact expected. Watch the interviewer
  (leaning forward, widened eyes) and check (*Do you follow?*; *Is there any part I can clarify?*).
  (S38)
- **Specific questions.** Self-introduction: steer the next question toward your strength (studies or
  work) and add one memorable personal detail. *What did you learn at university?*: how study
  changed your thinking and prepared you for graduate work. *Why should we choose you?*: fit,
  preparation, purpose, need, contribution, something unique: *a great investment*. Over-qualified
  for a fallback programme: scale your presentation to fit and leave other programmes unmentioned.
  *Where else are you applying?*: answer briefly. A hypothetical problem: answer as a
  mini-proposal (problem → challenges → solutions → plan). (S38)
- **After the interview.** Send thanks; follow up an incomplete answer; if unsuccessful, ask for
  constructive feedback (one applicant learned she failed because she presented herself as
  powerless to make a difference). If you miss one criterion (a test score), ask for conditional
  admission. Rehearse with seven different friends in the week before. (S38)

## 13. The research proposal (→ `annwuyn-paper`)

- **Why bother.** Many PhD and research master’s programmes and some scholarships require a
  proposal, and PhD programmes often expect one anyway. A strong proposal also opens professor
  contact: one student’s proposal drew positive replies from every professor she wrote to. Even
  a high-school applicant can design a small, sound pilot study. The wider value: a step-by-step
  way to analyse a problem and propose a solution, usable in interviews and at work. (S29)
- **Start small.** Novices should propose a replication: adapt a published study to a new context
  (a Spanish study of students’ English writing, replicated with Vietnamese students). Read
  widely first (the instructor suggests at least about 30 articles for an application proposal;
  the instructor’s own PhD proposal drew on about 300), starting from a few highly relevant papers and
  following their reference lists outward; prefer recent work, or you may retrace paths others
  have already walked. (S29, S33)
- **Sell, narrow, justify.** The introduction shows why the reader needs the study. Narrow the
  framework step by step and state the reason for each step (L1 influence on writing → textual
  features → Rheme-Theme linking only → argumentative essays only); “I just felt like it” is no
  reason. The literature review ends in the gap: methodological weaknesses a new design can fix,
  or a genre the field has neglected. (S29)
- **Questions that can be answered and that matter.** A research question must be specific and
  operationalizable: reading it, one should see the feasible study that answers it. *Do people go
  to parks or to motels more?* fails the “so what?” test; *dog walkers peak on weekend
  afternoons* needs a comparator and a problem it solves; *enhance the landscape* must become a
  measurable criterion (*green cover over three quarters of the park*). An overarching *how* question
  can split into testable sub-hypotheses whose answers are then synthesised. (S29)
- **Variables, plainly.** The independent variable is the one factor you investigate, with its
  values (grass sliding vs the 4D cinema are two values of one variable, the service); the
  dependent variable is the outcome you measure. Confounders (weather, a new blockbuster in 4D,
  a one-day 90% discount) must be controlled by design: choose conditions that hold them equal.
  A pre-test and post-test show gains, but any instruction raises scores, so add a control group
  matched at baseline; double-blind grading keeps raters from favouring a group. Bands for coding
  must follow one criterion (age bands defined by what the study needs; mixing “can go
  alone” with school levels breaks the scale). (S33)
- **Data handling.** Code entries consistently (one university, one code); check data entry by
  having two people enter the same records and comparing them (the lecture’s threshold: about 99%
  agreement). Analysis is field-specific; the lecture sketched choosing parametric or
  non-parametric tests by distribution. (S33)
- **Limitations, implications, abstract.** State what you could not control and why, and why you
  chose X over Y; state the expected contribution. The abstract (200 to 300 words) is placed first
  and written last; conferences often decide on it alone. (S33)
- **Reasoning puzzles in interviews.** *German infants take vitamin D until age two and Germans are
  taller, so all infants need it* confounds genes, nutrition and environment; a valid design
  would match groups on everything but the supplement until adulthood. Committees and employers
  use such puzzles to test design thinking. (S33)
- **Where current methodology refines the lecture.** Quantitative research measures and analyses
  numbers; many quantitative questions are descriptive and need no hypothesis, while inferential
  designs state hypotheses in advance. Qualitative research studies meaning and process; the
  “hypothesis or no hypothesis” test is a novice heuristic. Sample size comes from a power
  analysis or precision target (quantitative) or from saturation and information power
  (qualitative); copying the proportion that other studies sampled is no justification. Check
  model assumptions with plots: with large samples, normality tests flag trivial departures. See
  the `annwuyn-paper` skill and `13` for reporting standards. (S29, S33)

## 14. Engagement, argument, exam essays and fallacies (→ `06`, `09`)

- **What engagement changes.** Students call it the session that changes their argument writing
  most: a first draft “greedy in defending my own view… more a declaration than an argument”
  becomes cautious, self-questioning and polite. It takes long practice. (S35)
- **Fact, monogloss, heterogloss.** A fact can be measured or counted and really happened; a
  monoglossic claim keeps something subjective that numbers cannot prove; heterogloss shows
  through its resources. Monogloss is hard to spot, which is why writers produce so much of it
  unawares. Every word carries an intent: *still* means “you would not expect it, yet I still do”
  (Vietnamese *vẫn còn* suggests only time). Train by reading expert writers and asking which
  resource each word is and why. (S35)
- **Attribution and evidence.** Vietnamese model essays prove sweeping claims with sayings of the
  ancients (*ấu bất học, lão hà vi*); even endlessly repeated quotations from national leaders
  count only as common wisdom. Proverbs come in opposing pairs (*gần bùn mà chẳng hôi tanh mùi
  bùn* vs *gần mực thì đen*), so they prove nothing. In a timed test without sources, a
  pseudo-specific source is acceptable; IELTS tolerance of hearsay is no standard for academic
  writing. *A statement that could be considered illogical* hides the writer’s own judgement
  (→ *seems illogical to me*). (S34, S35)
- **Choose reporting verbs for what they signal.** *Demonstrate, prove, show, point out* endorse;
  *report, suggest, claim* attribute (*claim* even casts doubt). Swapping verbs as synonyms
  causes clashes: *I do not agree with the reasons cited by Brinkley for concluding…* grants his
  reasons the status it then denies; *as you pointed out* concedes the point being rebutted;
  *Brinkley proved…* followed by an attack confuses readers. Even native speakers need schooling
  to feel these differences. (S34)
- **Intensifiers betray doubt.** *I trust you* > *I really, really trust you*; *I’ll be there* > *I’ll
  definitely try*; *totally* and *absolutely* often mark an empty promise or a reluctant yes; a
  speaker who repeats “he deserved to win” three times is persuading himself. A US-returned
  student’s advice holds: *I believe* beats *I hold a strong belief*. In scholarship essays, state
  achievements without intensifiers and let numbers speak; in the passages on passion and plans,
  warmth is allowed. (S35)
- **Reason first, emotion by design.** In a rational dispute, emotion makes the arguer look amateur;
  some disputes (a courtroom appeal to fairness) are won by emotion, used deliberately. (S35)
- **Concede, then strike.** Grant what is true before rebutting (*While this is technically
  true…*; *Granted, building a new parking lot may be costly, but…*): yield one step, then answer.
  Online, Vietnamese arguers often state flat, harsh monogloss that a Western reader hears as
  shouting; the instructor’s own Vietnamese turned heteroglossic after years of English, and
  research on bilinguals reports that personality presentation shifts with language. (S34, S35)
- **Pronounce sparingly.** *It is clear to me that…*, *I contend…* push the writer forward as an
  authority; English argument plays down the arguer. Anger at an opponent drives learners into
  pronounce and harsh words; soften (*Some of your complaints seem valid*). (S34)
- **Toulmin in practice.** Claim and grounds sit in the text; warrant and backing lie hidden;
  qualifier and rebuttal grow out of exchange with an opponent. Rebut when evidence can refute the
  objection (a caption shows everyone was in the photo); qualify when it cannot (a brother born
  after the photo: *at the time the picture was taken*). Warrants are where fallacies hide and
  what test writers attack; a principle warrant (*once bitten, twice shy*) invites a rival
  principle (*never give up*), which demonstration of irrelevance answers. (S35)
- **Read a speaker’s argument.** A pageant contestant with under a minute produced claim, grounds,
  analogy warrant, anticipated objection and rebuttal (*it is a controlled substance, but once
  prescribed it is readily available*), qualified where needed and monoglossic where the point
  was obvious; another answered *How do they make you feel?* with feelings alone, and the
  interview scores followed. (S35)
- **Writing-skill and thinking exams.** TOEFL and IELTS test how you write (fixed form, mechanics);
  GRE and LSAT test what you argue (flexible form, evidence, anticipation, fallacies); template
  preparation suits the first and misleads for the second. IELTS 8.5 and above, by teachers’
  reports, also rewards argument quality. The research-writing view of a typical IELTS prompt
  (*young people spend too much money following trends*) would first define *young people* and
  *too much*; IELTS does not ask for that, so IELTS writing does not transfer to journal writing
  without new learning. (S36)
- **Exam craft.** Five minutes of outlining (choose the side easier to argue, draw the idea map, draft
  the opening lines), twenty of writing, five of revising; in format-led exams write the
  conclusion right after the introduction. Make the introduction mirror the body structure; add
  no new information at the end (the Vietnamese “open ending” has no place in English argument).
  One extended example beats many short ones. Sprinkle vocabulary and idioms at the revision
  stage (*rắc hành*): it shows range with few misuses. (S35, S36)
- **Essay types and structure.** Argumentative (how the world is) vs persuasive (how it should be);
  one-sided vs two-sided. In problem-solution essays, group solutions when problems share one,
  pair them when each is independent. In compare-and-contrast essays use blocks (A, then B, then
  the evaluation), because alternating paragraphs lose linearity. A prompt open to two readings
  (*do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?*) takes the structure of the reading you choose.
  (S35)
- **Critical-reasoning items: fallacy first.** Read the whole stimulus, name its main fallacy, check
  whether the task is to strengthen or weaken, and only then scan the options: the answer shows at
  once (a test road generalised to a state: sampling error; *if souls are immortal, evil will be
  punished*: sufficiency and necessity; whole-market sales vs one firm’s sales: composition).
  Trawling five options in translation is how 73% of candidates miss a hard item. (S36, S37)
- **Evaluating an argument.** Split it into sub-arguments and pick the two you can attack most fully:
  for each, data → claim → warrant → why it fails → what evidence would repair it. Explain the
  faulty reasoning in plain words; fallacy labels vary between sources and prove nothing. The
  GRE has dropped this essay, but the skill serves critical-reasoning sections, peer review and
  research critique. (S37)
- **Fallacies the course adds to the table.** Appeal to pity (*the only sleep I got was driving to
  work*) and appeal to force. Straw man most often enters exam essays through paraphrasing the
  prompt into an easier, different issue. Red herring and straw man differ formally: a straw man
  recasts X as X′ and refutes X′; a red herring argues a related Y, which proves nothing about X.
  *In the absence of evidence to the contrary* slides into burden of proof when it means “no
  counter-evidence, so true”. Composition shows in data analysis: compare rates (visits per
  person per week); raw totals mislead. We all commit these fallacies; spotting them in others’
  writing is the training. (S36, S37)

## 15. Where this library departs from the lectures

**Integrity.** The plugin never helps a user misrepresent anything. Advice given in class that it
replaces:

- Letters signed by someone whose stated role (supervisor, homeroom teacher) is untrue, on the
  grounds that schools rarely phone referees (S21): replaced by the fact-sheet method in §12.
- Inventing a story, a difficulty or an analysis “because nobody can check” (L4, L5, L7), or
  inflating a project’s scale: describe what actually happened; if a method was learned but not
  yet applied, say so and show how you would apply it.
- Translations that upgrade a record (shifting each Vietnamese degree classification one level up)
  or rename a role to hide its nature (S38): use official English names and add the grading
  explanation; describe roles accurately.
- Choosing an event’s label by the outcome (*contest* if you won, *conference* if you did not):
  name the event as it is called and state what you did.
- The comic euphemisms in the language-use slides (bullying as “training children in survival
  skills”) are jokes; the plugin does not use them.

**Facts and usage, checked in November 2026.**

- Singular *they*: endorsed by APA Style (7th ed., §4.18) and *The Chicago Manual of Style* (18th
  ed., ¶5.51) for a person whose gender is unknown or unstated (§4 above).
- *Can’t* in *That can’t be Tom* is epistemic in standard grammars; corpus studies report a decline
  of deontic *must* (§10 above).
- *Assumption* (Attribute) vs *supposition* (Endorse): learner dictionaries define *supposition* as
  an idea believed to be true without proof, so the pairing is unreliable. For a testable claim
  use *hypothesis*; for an endorsed one, *finding* or *evidence*.
- *Conclude* as an Endorse verb depends on whether the writer accepts the conclusion. Martin and
  White’s Endorse examples centre on *show, demonstrate, prove, find, point out*; *indeed* and
  *really* are Pronounce, so for concession use *admittedly* or *granted*.
- Toulmin’s own qualifier marks the force of the step from data to claim, and his rebuttal states
  the conditions of exception; the course’s “rebuttal” (counterargument plus answer) follows
  composition textbooks. Both senses are useful; name which one you mean.
- The conformity experiment shown in class: in Asch’s studies about a third of critical responses
  conformed and about three quarters of participants conformed at least once.
- *You would submit your paper today* is offered in class as a polite but firm order; native
  speakers would say *Please submit your paper today* or *I need your paper today*.
- Address professors by title and surname until they invite first names.
- Test formats changed after the recordings: see `09` and `13` §10.
- The instructor’s IELTS 8.0 target is a personal recommendation for coping with graduate
  coursework; programmes set their own minimums.
- Telegraphic CV-style fragments (*Strong background in math and statistics. Valedictorian…*) suit
  character-limited fields only; use full sentences elsewhere.
- Corpus percentages quoted in class (modal ratios, academic-vocabulary shares) were stated
  orally; treat them as rough guides.
