# 11 — Vietnamese L1 transfer: the diagnostic checklist

Source: this library throughout; S1–S2 (Kaplan, contrastive rhetoric), S8 (Hoàng Ngọc Tuấn & Nguyễn
Hưng Quốc), S19 (Nguyễn Hạnh on SOPs), S34 (Engagement frequencies).

This is the list to run against any draft written by a Vietnamese L1 writer. Every item is
attested in the course materials, not inferred.

## The premise

Kaplan (1966): *There are underlying rhetorical patterns common to all written languages.
Languages, however, differ in the frequency these patterns occur.*
And the course's own qualification: **pattern of thought ≠ pattern of text development**;
underlying coherence ≠ surface cohesion.

English expository prose is **dominantly linear**: topic statement → subdivisions, each
supported by example → related to the whole, to prove or argue something (or the inductive
reverse). *Maturity of style in English is gauged by degree of subordination rather than
coordination.*

Vietnamese argumentative prose, by the Vietnamese critics' own account: writers rarely go
straight to the point, rarely divide thought into distinct points, rarely arrange those points
into a consistent tight structure, rarely state their evaluation clearly, rarely land a
conclusion that reads as the end of a completed line of reasoning. Loose structure, weak
inter-idea relations, little sense of paragraphing or of key sentences. The aesthetic values
ambiguity and indirectness — *vừa viết vừa lách*.

**English is a writer-responsible language.** The burden of making the link is on you.

## Structural checklist

1. **Non-linearity** — the seven criteria in `01-linearity.md`. Especially: gaps,
   repetition (same idea different wording), and bi-directionality.
2. **Over-reliance on implicit links** — see `05-linking-facilitation.md`. Symptoms: linking
   words that look wrong, arguments that seem "not tight enough", cross-cultural
   miscommunication.
3. **Superficial force-linking with a convenient conjunction** — *Besides*, *Moreover*,
   *Therefore* dropped in where the chain does not connect.
4. **Unwarranted marked Themes** — significantly more frequent than in American writing, and
   mostly disruptive.
5. **Theme shift / Rheme-Rheme redundancy / referent shift** — see `02-thematic-progression.md`.
6. **Word-level linearity** — *other better services in Asian countries* → *other Asian
   countries*; *most favorite*; *consider dogs as*.
7. **2-verb Vietnamese sequences** carried into English — downgrade one to a modifier
   (`04-dependency.md`).
8. **PP-modification of nouns** where English wants an NP (`08-economy.md`).
9. **Redundancy from Sino-Vietnamese/native doublets** (`08-economy.md`).

## Rhetorical / stance checklist

10. **Monogloss over-use and bare assertions** — Vietnamese and ESL ≫ American. Assumes reader
    like-mindedness; risks unfounded statements.
11. **OPINION over POSTULATION** — imposing subjective views rather than presenting objective
    observation.
12. **COMMON WISDOM over SPECIFIC SOURCE** — proverbs and *người xưa đã nhắc nhở…* in place of
    citable sources.
13. **Under-qualification** — unqualified reads as absolute. Also **over-qualification** in the
    other direction.
14. **SHOULD instead of MUST** to sound polite — produces a lecturing tone.
15. **Hedging where assertion is called for** — in SOPs and LORs. *I hope that this will enable
    me…*, *could be judged as*, *I do not think I will have any difficulties* (negation of a
    negative is humble in Vietnamese style, weak in English).
16. **Warrants left implicit** — the main fallacy risk.
17. **Manner of exemplification** — many unexpanded examples instead of few elaborated ones;
    rationales presented as examples.

## Application-genre checklist (Nguyễn Hạnh, VN vs AM SOPs)

18. **Personal information up front** — age, family size, birth order. Normal in Vietnamese
    interaction; absent from American essays.
19. **No ORIENTATION TO FIELD** — present in 75 % of AM essays, 1 of the VN essays. When VN
    writers do explain it, they construct the self as **passive recipient** (*My father was a
    teacher of English. He taught me English…*) where AM writers show **active interest**
    (*I began to study foreign languages in the eighth grade and continued…*).
20. **No specific sub-fields** named for the target program → reads as unprepared.
21. **Less assertive presentation of achievements** — see 15.
22. **No PROGRAM APPRAISAL / self-matching** — AM writers place themselves in a **two-way
    selection**; VN writers in a one-way selection, hoping only to be chosen.
23. **Program framed as a solution to a deficit** — the applicant's or the community's — rather
    than a match of interests. A request model, absent from AM essays.
24. **BENEFITS framed communally** — *serve my university, my country and my community*; AM
    writers state what they personally gain. Both are legitimate (and community benefit matters
    for ODA scholarships) — but know which you are doing and why.
25. **SELF-PROMOTION as fixed traits, not processes** — VN writers list achievements as
    *product*; AM writers show a **problem–solution sequence** in which they were the active
    agent, then offer their own interpretation of what it meant.
    → *focus on processes, adding personal interpretations and self-criticism*:
    problem identification → challenges → specific solutions → results (in perspective) →
    implications & lessons learned.

## The remedies, in order

1. Fix linearity first. Always. It repairs linking, dependency, grammar, paraphrasing,
   redundancy and sentence complexity at once.
2. Then TP.
3. Then cohesion and dependency.
4. Then scope, distance, explicitness.
5. Then engagement, modality, qualification.
6. Then economy of language.

Caveat from the course: **do not take shortcuts yet.** Local violations of linearity are
legitimate trade-offs *after* the base chain is right, not instead of it. And expect the
**U-shaped learning curve** and the **intermediate plateau** — writing often gets worse before
it gets better, as attention shifts from surface accuracy to structure.
