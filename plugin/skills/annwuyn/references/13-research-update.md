## 0. The premise, updated: intercultural rhetoric (→ `11`)

Kaplan (1966) is now read critically. Critics showed that it treats national cultures as fixed
thought patterns and takes Anglo-American prose as the yardstick (Atkinson, 2004; Kubota &
Lehner, 2004). The field’s current name is **intercultural rhetoric** (Connor, 2004, 2011):
rhetorical preferences belong to genres, disciplines, school traditions and discourse
communities; they change over time and are negotiated text by text. Le Ha (2009) gives a
Vietnamese scholar’s account of that negotiation in the author’s own English academic writing.

In practice:

- Run `11-vn-transfer.md` as a list of **tendencies to test in this draft**. Report what the
  draft does, with the sentence that shows it. The writer’s L1 tells you where to look; the
  draft supplies the finding.
- Present Anglophone academic conventions as the conventions of the target readers: a genre,
  learnable like any other. The course says the same thing in its own words: *pattern of
  thought ≠ pattern of text development*.
- Kaplan’s line *maturity of style in English is gauged by degree of subordination* no longer
  describes research prose. See §3.

## 1. Linearity and information flow (→ `01`, `02`)

- **Old information first, new information last.** Readers expect the start of a sentence to
  link back to what they know (topic position) and the end to carry the new, important point
  (stress position) (Gopen & Swan, 1990; Williams & Bizup, 2017). This is the reader-side
  account of the course’s RT pattern. Use it to explain TP to a writer who has never met
  Halliday.
- **Characters as subjects, actions as verbs** (Williams & Bizup, 2017). A subject that is a
  nominalised action (*The implementation of the intervention was…*) usually hides who did
  what. Weigh it against §3 before rewriting.
- **The curse of knowledge** (Pinker, 2014): writers find it hard to imagine a reader who lacks
  what they know. It is the cognitive root of the three causes of over-implicit linking in
  `05` §C. The test: hand the draft to a reader from outside the field.
- **Varied progression.** Hawes (2015) compared essays by students entering an MA with prose
  from two British newspapers. He recommends teaching more variants of Daneš’s progression
  types and more “breaks” (non-participant Themes) to mark rhetorical shifts. In course terms:
  a motivated N transition is a resource; only U is a failure state.
- **Context, content, conclusion at every level** (Mensh & Kording, 2017): each paragraph, each
  section and the whole paper open with context, deliver the content and close on the
  conclusion, around one central contribution. This is the course’s sub-grouping model applied
  from the paragraph up to the paper.

## 2. Cohesion (→ `03`, `linking-items.md`)

- **Which connector, and how.** Learner essays use about as many connectors as native essays;
  the trouble lies in individual items, overused, underused or misused semantically,
  stylistically and syntactically. The authors’ teaching advice matches the course: teach the
  behaviour of each connector from authentic texts and give out no lists of “interchangeable”
  connectors (Granger & Tyson, 1996). `linking-items.md` is that tool.
- **Attended *this*.** Pronoun *this/these* in published research articles mostly points back
  to the single preceding clause (Gray, 2010). When the antecedent is longer or contested,
  attach a summary noun: *This decline…*, *This approach…*. In student writing the clusters
  *this is* and *this means* are turning into text-organising markers (Wulff et al., 2012), so a
  bare *this* at a paragraph turn is acceptable when its antecedent is the whole preceding
  sentence.
- **Metadiscursive nouns** (*this analysis*, *the goal*, *this finding*) carry cohesion and
  stance at once, and their use is changing across disciplines (Jiang & Hyland, 2021).

## 3. Dependency and compression (→ `04`, `08`)

Corpus research has revised the old view that academic English is complex because of clause
subordination. Research prose is **compressed**: its complexity sits inside noun phrases
(attributive adjectives, nouns as premodifiers, prepositional phrases as postmodifiers). Finite
dependent clauses are typical of conversation (Biber & Gray, 2010, 2016). Biber et al. (2011)
propose a developmental order for writers: finite dependent clauses, then non-finite clauses,
then phrasal modification of nouns.

In practice:

- The course’s NP and affix principles (`08` §4 and §5) describe what expert writers do.
- Compression costs explicitness: a stacked noun phrase (*teacher technology-integration
  self-efficacy scores*) hides the relations between its parts (Biber & Gray, 2010). Unpack it
  wherever a novice, or a reviewer from a neighbouring field, has to follow.
- Downgrading (`04`) and NP compression reach the same goal by two routes. Keep a clause where
  the relation matters (cause, concession, condition). Use the noun phrase where the relation
  is obvious to the target reader.

## 4. Engagement and stance (→ `06`, `07`)

- **Hyland’s stance and engagement model** (Hyland, 2005, 2019) is the standard partner of
  Martin and White in English for academic purposes. Stance: hedges, boosters, attitude
  markers, self-mention. Engagement: reader mentions, directives, questions, appeals to shared
  knowledge, personal asides. Use its labels when the user’s field cites Hyland.
- **Explicit stance has fallen.** In top journals from 1965 to 2015, hedges per 10,000 words
  fell 36% in applied linguistics and 21% in sociology; boosters fell 38% and 35%.
  Self-mention rose 63% in electrical engineering and 163% in biology, and fell 27% in applied
  linguistics. *Suggest* and *likely* were the only frequent hedges to grow (Hyland & Jiang,
  2016). Current prose qualifies with fewer, better-placed hedges, which sharpens the course’s
  warning on over-qualification (`07` §B).
- **Slightly more informal, in the sciences:** first-person pronouns, unattended reference and
  sentence-initial conjunctions (Hyland & Jiang, 2017). *We* in Methods and Discussion is
  normal in most fields; check the target journal.
- **Hype is rising.** Positive words in PubMed titles and abstracts rose from 2.0% (1974 to
  1980) to 17.5% (2014), with *robust*, *novel*, *innovative* and *unprecedented* leading
  (Vinkers et al., 2015). In 901,717 successful NIH grant abstracts (1985 to 2020), 130
  promotional adjectives grew; *novel*, *critical* and *key* grew most in absolute terms
  (Millar et al., 2022; see also Hyland & Jiang, 2021). Treat these words as Pronounce
  resources (`06` target 4): keep one only where the evidence carries it.
- **Machine-written argument is flat.** Essays written by ChatGPT carry fewer engagement
  markers (questions and personal asides above all), less epistemic stance and authorial
  presence, and more rigid noun- and preposition-based bundles than student essays (Jiang &
  Hyland, 2025a, 2025b). A draft that reads this way needs Engagement targets 1 to 5 in `06`.

## 5. Modality, qualification and claims about evidence (→ `07`, `09`)

- **Statistics.** A *p*-value does not measure the size of an effect or its importance, and
  *p* > .05 is no evidence of a null effect (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016; Wasserstein et al.,
  2019; Amrhein et al., 2019). Report effect sizes with confidence intervals. Write
  *statistically significant* in full when the term is used at all, and describe a
  non-significant result as compatible with the range of effects in its interval.
- **Causal language.** Replacing *cause* with *is associated with* does not make a claim
  clearer. Reviewers rated *associated* as at least somewhat causal, and in 44.5% of sampled
  articles the action recommendations implied more causality than the result sentences
  (Haber et al., 2022). When the aim is causal, say so, name the assumptions that license the
  answer and keep recommendations at the strength of the design (Hernán, 2018; Labrecque &
  Kezios, 2026). This refines the course’s Correlation fallacy in `09`.
- **Spin.** Conclusions that outrun the primary results are common in abstracts and press
  releases (Boutron & Ravaud, 2018; Chiu et al., 2017). Check every abstract conclusion
  against the primary outcome.

## 6. Economy and readability (→ `08`)

- Abstracts have become harder to read since 1881, driven by general scientific jargon
  (Plavén-Sigray et al., 2017, 709,577 abstracts). More jargon in the title and abstract
  predicts fewer citations (Martínez & Mammola, 2021). Shorter titles (Letchford et al., 2015)
  and abstracts in more frequent words (Letchford et al., 2016) earn slightly more citations.
  The effects are small and point the same way as `08`.
- Narrative features in abstracts (sensory language, connectivity, appeals to the reader) were
  linked to more citations in climate science (Hillier et al., 2016).
- **The Paramedic Method** (Lanham, 2007) is a quick check after stage 8: circle the
  prepositions and the forms of *be*, find the action, put it in a simple active verb, start
  fast, read the sentence aloud.
- **Plain-language summaries.** Guidance converges on short sentences, common words, active
  voice and terms defined on first use (Stoll et al., 2022). The `08` toolkit produces them.

## 7. Vocabulary and formulas (→ `10`, `12`)

- Word lists: the Academic Word List (Coxhead, 2000) and the newer Academic Vocabulary List
  (Gardner & Davies, 2014). Multi-word formulas: the Academic Formulas List (Simpson-Vlach &
  Ellis, 2010). Lexical bundles differ by discipline (Hyland, 2008), so the field’s own corpus
  outranks a general list when the two disagree.
- **Words that now read as machine-processed.** After large language models arrived, a set of
  style words jumped in frequency in biomedical abstracts: rare words led by *delves*,
  *underscores* and *showcasing*; common words led by *potential*, *findings* and *crucial*,
  with *across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably,
  particularly, within* as a marker set. At least 13.5% of 2024 PubMed abstracts were
  processed with an LLM, up to about 40% in some subcorpora (Kobak et al., 2025). Across arXiv,
  bioRxiv and Nature-portfolio papers, LLM-modified text reached up to 22% in computer science
  by September 2024 (Liang et al., 2025). Editors and reviewers know these lists. Use the plain
  word, and run the humanizer passes.

## 8. Research writing (→ `annwuyn-paper`)

- **Introduction moves (CARS).** Swales (1990, 2004): Move 1 establishes a territory, Move 2
  establishes a niche (a gap, or an addition to what is known), Move 3 presents the present
  work (aims or questions, method in brief, main outcome, value, outline). Swales and Feak
  (2012) teach it step by step; a fourth edition is announced for 2027.
- **Gap or problem.** Gap-spotting (something is confused, neglected or untested) produces
  incremental questions. Problematization identifies an assumption in the literature and
  challenges it, and tends to produce more interesting questions (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011;
  Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011). Every gap statement must also say why the gap matters to the
  reader: *I am studying X because I want to find out Y, in order to help my reader understand
  Z* (Booth et al., 2024).
- **Entering the conversation.** Summarise what *they say* before *I say*, and mark where one
  ends and the other begins (Graff & Birkenstein, 2024; the sixth edition adds a chapter on
  generative AI as a source).
- **Question frameworks.** FINER (feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant) for any
  question, and PICOT for intervention questions (Thabane et al., 2009); SPIDER for
  qualitative and mixed-methods review questions (Cooke et al., 2012). They complement the course’s
  *specific and operationalizable* rule.
- **Reporting guidelines** (EQUATOR Network): APA JARS for quantitative (Appelbaum et al.,
  2018), qualitative and mixed-methods research (Levitt et al., 2018); CONSORT 2025 for trials,
  30 items with a new open-science section (Hopewell et al., 2025); SPIRIT 2025 for trial
  protocols, 34 items including patient and public involvement (Chan et al., 2025); PRISMA
  2020 for systematic reviews (Page et al., 2021); RTARG for reflexive thematic analysis
  (Braun & Clarke, 2024). In reflexive thematic analysis, themes are constructed by the
  researcher, so the report says who developed them (*we developed three themes*); *themes
  emerged* misdescribes the method (Braun & Clarke, 2021).
- **Open science statements.** Preregistration (Nosek et al., 2018), Registered Reports
  (Chambers & Tzavella, 2022), contributor roles in CRediT (Brand et al., 2015), and a data
  availability statement. Many journals now require one or more of these.
- **Abstracts and clarity.** Structured abstracts are more informative and easier to read and
  search (Hartley, 2014). Gernsbacher (2018) gives practical rules for clear, memorable
  empirical articles: consistent terms, short sentences, concrete examples, few abbreviations.
- **Learning to publish.** Belcher (2019) is a week-by-week revision programme built around
  making the argument explicit.

## 9. AI use and disclosure (→ every mode)

- ICMJE: AI tools cannot be authors because they cannot take responsibility for the work;
  authors disclose AI use at submission, in the cover letter and in the relevant section;
  humans stay accountable for every sentence; AI output cannot be cited as a primary source;
  undisclosed use may be treated as misconduct (ICMJE, 2025).
- COPE: disclose AI use in writing, images or data work in the Methods or a similar section;
  authors are liable for AI-produced content (COPE, 2023).
- When annwuyn edits a manuscript, remind the user once of the target journal’s disclosure
  rule. Admissions offices set their own rules for SOPs and personal statements; check the
  programme’s page.

## 10. Applications and tests (→ `annwuyn-scholarship`, `09`)

- **Doubt raisers in letters.** Negative language, hedges, unexplained comments, faint praise
  and irrelevant detail lower a letter’s value, and they appear more often in letters for
  women (Trix & Psenka, 2003; Madera et al., 2019). Letters for women also carry more communal
  and fewer agentic or standout words (Madera et al., 2009; Dutt et al., 2016). Check every
  draft letter for both patterns.
- **Test formats changed** (checked on the test makers’ pages):
  - GRE General Test: since 22 September 2023 one writing task only, *Analyze an Issue*
    (30 minutes); *Analyze an Argument* was removed (ETS, 2023).
  - TOEFL iBT: since 21 January 2026 Writing has *Build a Sentence*, *Write an Email* and
    *Write for an Academic Discussion* (12 items, 23 minutes); scores on a 1 to 6 band scale,
    with a 0 to 120 equivalent during a two-year transition (ETS, 2026).
  - LSAT Argumentative Writing: since 30 July 2024, 15 minutes of guided prewriting analysis
    and 35 minutes for an argumentative essay built from several sources of evidence (LSAC,
    2024).
  - GMAT Focus Edition (from 7 November 2023): no essay section; critical reasoning remains in
    Verbal Reasoning.
- **Labels for countries and groups.** APA’s *Inclusive Language Guide* (2023) calls “third
  world” and “developing” countries terms that “are no longer accurate in categorizing
  countries”; the World Bank stopped using “developing world” in its data (Khokhar &
  Serajuddin, 2015). Use a checkable label: the World Bank income group, the region or the
  country’s name.
